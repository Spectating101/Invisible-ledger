## Chapter 1
In 2023, Indonesia's figures moved away from the commerce they stand for.
This thesis calls what a revenue-only measure leaves out the invisible wedge.
It does not say when a figure stops moving with the activity it represents, or by how much.
Measuring what the current figures miss begins with one question: how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth?
H1 says that a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value.
H2 says that national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online, and less from higher sales per business.
In brief: revenue shows only a small part of the commerce platforms carry, and marketplaces carry only about a fifth of online sales value in BPS's survey.
But about half of the 2023 rise in the count of sellers is not explained by first-time entry.
## Chapter 2
Each body of research explains why the figures differ, but none measures how far they differ in practice.
Revenue is a fair guide to the commerce only if it keeps a steady share of that commerce.
So revenue can rise when a platform raises its fees or cuts its discounts, with no change in what customers buy.
Testing whether revenue stays a fair guide needs the reported revenue and transaction value of the same platforms, over several years and on one definition.
Among the studies reviewed here, it has not been applied to platform commerce and national online sales.
Published totals cannot show whether a rise in the count reflects new sellers or a wider survey, because they do not record when each seller began.
How far a rule built on platform records can reach depends on how much selling happens on platforms.
## Chapter 3
Revenue sits inside platform commerce, because a platform keeps only part of each sale.
Platform commerce sits inside all online commerce, because many sellers trade outside platforms.
Growth divergence carries the central test: whether revenue and transaction value keep the same relationship from year to year.
So a large ecosystem ratio means that ordinary changes in fees or discounts can produce large swings in reported revenue growth.
The survey can, because it asks every seller in which year it began selling online.
The threshold is the smallest share of new sellers that can produce the rise.
## Chapter 4
The figures this thesis compares come from sources that were never designed to be read together.
Setting the two sides against each other for one country is what makes the tests possible.
Each platform gets one number, the median size of its yearly changes, because the pairs of one platform are not independent of one another.
A registered test was written down, dated and stored in the repository before the data were opened.
An exploratory test was designed after some results were seen.
A descriptive result reports numbers without a test.
## Chapter 5
Revenue showed only a small part of what Indonesia's major platforms carried.
Revenue did not keep pace with transaction value at the Indonesian and regional platforms, and the thesis rejects H1.
The main test was set up after a first, narrower test, so it is exploratory.
So the rejection of H1 describes that period.
Pricing, not more commerce, moved revenue at the Indonesian and regional platforms.
Higher fees and lower incentives together outweighed more commerce in every window.
Across 28 listed platforms and 138 platform-years, market value moved with transaction value, a registered result.
The second registered prediction does not hold in the main sample.
Buying through platforms did not boom in 2023.
H2 holds on BPS's published figures, but the registered entry test shows that first-time entry cannot explain the whole rise in the count.
The registered entry test separates new sellers from sellers counted for the first time, and it fails.
About half of the 2023 rise in the count is not explained by first-time entry, an exploratory result.
H2 holds on the published series, but it sits right on its threshold once the extra count is read as coverage, an exploratory follow-up.
The extra count leaned toward long-running businesses, older owners and places outside Java, an exploratory result.
Returning sellers still cannot be ruled out.
Most online selling happens outside marketplaces, and records are thinner there.
The national figures agree that growth was small only where they cover the same sellers, and they do not agree on scale.
## Chapter 6
Each rule acts on one part of what Chapter 5 measured, and each reaches only that part.
It can collect from at most about 1 in 20 online sellers and about a fifth of online sales value.
The commission cap reaches a small part of the take rates this thesis measures.
A cap on one fee does not fix the take rate, because the platform still sets its incentives.
At most about 1 in 20 online sellers falls under the tax.
The tax matters more for the record it creates than for the revenue it raises.
Every one of these rules reaches only the sales that pass through a platform.
## Chapter 7
Each figure of Indonesia's online economy is a fair guide to another only while a condition holds.
The answer to the first half is that most of the activity remains outside revenue.
The answer to the second half differs by link.
On the outer link, H2 holds on BPS's published figures: more businesses account for most of the growth in online sales value.
Several registered predictions failed, and the failures are part of the answer.
The invisible wedge is the part of each sale that a platform passes on to sellers, drivers and others.
It is not missing output, unrecorded income or unpaid tax.
A reader who asks which of those happened can still use all three.
