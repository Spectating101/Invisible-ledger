# Chapter 2. Literature Review

This thesis asks when a figure stops following the activity it is meant to describe. A platform's revenue is one such figure, and it is meant to describe the commerce the platform carries. A national count of online sellers is another, and it is meant to describe how many businesses sell online. Judging such figures, and what follows from them, takes three kinds of knowledge. Each comes from a different part of the literature.

The first is why a figure and its activity can differ at all. For platform revenue, the answer lies in how platforms set prices and how accountants record sales. The second is how else the activity can be measured, so that a figure has something to be checked against. Here the literature covers national accounts, business surveys and the methods behind them. The third is how the records are used. Governments now write tax rules on platform records, and what a rule can reach depends on what the records hold.

The chapter has one section for each part. Section 2.1 explains what a platform is and how it earns and reports revenue. Section 2.2 explains how online selling is measured and what can go wrong in the measure. Section 2.3 explains how platform records and information from third parties are used in tax. Each section ends by stating what it shows and what it leaves out. Section 2.4 collects what is left out into the questions this thesis answers.

## 2.1 What a platform is and how it earns revenue

A platform is a business that connects two groups and helps them deal with each other. Tokopedia connects shoppers with merchants, and Grab connects riders with drivers. Platforms such as these do not make or own what is sold. Platform economics is the field that studies how such businesses set prices and earn money. Its starting point is that a platform serves two groups at once, so it must price them together (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Evans and Schmalensee 2016).

Platform pricing explains why a platform's revenue need not follow the commerce it carries. A platform needs both groups to join. If few sellers join, buyers have little reason to come, and the same holds the other way round. So a platform may charge one group little or nothing and earn from the other. How much it earns then depends on its price structure as well as on the commerce it carries (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Armstrong 2006; Evans and Schmalensee 2016). The business model matters as well. A marketplace earns a fee from each sale, while a reseller buys goods and sells them on (Hagiu and Wright 2015). The reseller's revenue is the whole sale, and the marketplace's is the fee.

Accounting rules decide how much of each sale becomes revenue. Revenue recognition is the name for these rules. International Financial Reporting Standard 15 (IFRS 15) is the international standard for it. Under IFRS 15, a company acting as a principal reports the full payment from the customer as revenue. A company acting as an agent reports only what it earns from arranging the sale (International Accounting Standards Board 2014). Payments that a company makes to its own customers, such as discounts and vouchers, can reduce its reported revenue (International Accounting Standards Board 2014). A study of internet firms found that they chose different ways of reporting revenue, including whether to report sales gross (Bowen, Davis and Rajgopal 2002). So fees, customer incentives, accounting choices, service mix, acquisitions and changes in business scope can all move reported revenue without an equal change in commerce.

Revenue is not the only reported figure whose meaning depends on how it is computed. Analysts use financial statements more easily when the accounting is comparable (De Franco, Kothari and Verdi 2011). Measures that companies choose to report, such as transaction value or active users, lose information when their definition changes or goes unreported (Givoly, Li, Lourie and Nekrasov 2019). Investors also look beyond revenue. In the early years of the internet, activity measures such as web traffic carried information about a firm's value and future revenue that reported revenue did not show (Trueman, Wong and Zhang 2000, 2001; Rajgopal, Venkatachalam and Kotha 2003).

Platform economics and accounting research explain why transaction value can exceed revenue and why that difference can move. They do not measure how large it is for platforms in a market such as Indonesia, or whether it stays steady from year to year.

## 2.2 How online selling is measured

E-commerce means selling goods or services over the internet. No single number measures it, because different groups count different things. Listed platforms report the revenue they record and often their transaction value. Statistics offices survey businesses and report their online sales. Central banks report the value of payments. Industry reports estimate the size of whole markets from platform transaction values (Google, Temasek and Bain 2024; BPS 2025; Bank Indonesia 2025). Chapter 4 describes each source for Indonesia.

Official frameworks already separate the commerce a platform carries from the service the platform provides. National accounts, the statistics that add up a country's production, income and spending, treat the sale between buyer and seller as distinct from the platform's intermediation service. They do not count the full transaction value as the platform's output (Ahmad and Schreyer 2016; International Monetary Fund 2018; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD] 2023; United Nations et al. 2025).

The sources do not give the same number. The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) has shown that e-commerce values change with the definition used. It has also set business e-commerce sales beside the transaction values of online platforms (UNCTAD 2023, 2024). A difference between figures is harmless while they move together. It matters when they part, because the choice of figure can then change the conclusion about scale and growth. Economists who check national income figures against satellite images of night-time lights follow the same logic: a figure should be tested against the activity it stands for (Henderson, Storeygard and Weil 2012).

The growth of national online sales has two possible sources, which makes it a figure worth testing. It can come from existing sellers selling more or from more sellers entering, and so it can be split between the two. Trade economists divide the growth of exports in the same way (Hummels and Klenow 2005).

The split of national growth rests on a count of online sellers, and that count has a weakness of its own. It depends on how the survey finds sellers. A change in design can break a series in a way that looks like a change in the economy (van den Brakel, Smith and Compton 2008; van den Brakel, Zhang and Tam 2020). Respondents also misdate events, and they tend to place them closer to the interview than they were (Neter and Waksberg 1964). Exit among small firms is more common for young firms and young owners (McKenzie and Paffhausen 2019), which matters when a count has to be corrected for sellers who stopped.

Research on measurement shows that official frameworks treat commerce and platform service as different things. It shows that figures from different sources need not agree, and that a seller count can break when a survey changes. It does not show whether platform and national figures move together in a given country, or what a rise in a seller count contains.

## 2.3 Platform records and third-party information

A platform keeps a record of every sale: who sold, what, for how much and when. Third-party information is a report about a taxpayer made by someone else, such as an employer, a bank or a platform. Tax authorities value it because it does not depend on the taxpayer's own account.

Platform records can help where other records are thin. Sellers and service providers may be small, self-employed or weakly represented in conventional financial records, yet the platform records what they sell. This differs from the shadow-economy setting, where reliable records may be sparse (La Porta and Shleifer 2014; Ulyssea 2018). Digital records can carry information that conventional records lack (Berg, Burg, Gombovic and Puri 2020), and platform-mediated work leaves traces in financial and administrative data (Barrios, Hochberg and Yi 2022; Denes, Lagaras and Tsoutsoura 2025).

Information from a third party also changes how people comply with tax. Audit experiments in Denmark, and studies of the value-added tax and of receipt lotteries, show that reports from third parties help compliance (Kleven et al. 2011; Pomeranz 2015; Naritomi 2019; Kleven, Kreiner and Saez 2016; Slemrod 2019). Tax withheld through a third party rests on the same information (Brockmeyer and Hernandez 2016).

Several rules now apply third-party reporting to platforms. The OECD, a group of mostly high-income countries, published model rules under which platforms report what their sellers earn (OECD 2020). The European Union's DAC7 directive applies them (European Union 2021). Indonesia's marketplace tax rule goes further: it makes marketplaces collect tax from their sellers, not only report on them (Ministry of Finance of the Republic of Indonesia 2025).

Research on third-party information shows that reports from third parties help compliance, and that platform rules apply the idea. It does not show how many of a country's online sellers such a rule can reach, or how much of the market platform records cover.

## 2.4 The research gap

Platform economics, accounting, measurement and tax research each answer their own question. None measures how closely platform figures follow the activity behind them, or whether national figures do. Three questions remain.

1. **How far does a platform's revenue follow the commerce it carries?** Section 2.1 gives reasons why the two can part, but not when or by how much.
2. **Do platform figures follow national growth?** Section 2.2 shows that figures can differ. The answer depends on where national growth comes from and on whether a count of sellers keeps pace with entry.
3. **How far do rules built on platform records reach?** Section 2.3 shows that such records can support tax rules, but not how much of the online economy they cover.

Chapter 3 turns the three questions into measures and hypotheses.
