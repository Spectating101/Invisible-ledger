# Chapter 2. Literature Review

Chapter 1 asked how much of an online economy is missing when a platform is measured by its revenue, and when that changes the story about growth. No single study answers it, but four lines of research each answer a piece. Platform economics explains why a platform keeps only a share of what its users trade. Accounting explains what a company may record as revenue. National statistics explain how the commerce of a whole country is counted. Tax research explains what a platform's own records can show. This chapter sets out what each line concludes, uses it to answer the question as well as it can, and shows where the answer breaks down. The breaking points are the gap that this thesis fills.

A case from Indonesia shows how the four lines differ. In 2023 Tokopedia's revenue rose by 53 percent while the value of the sales it carried fell by 9 percent. Platform economics would look at how the platform prices the two groups it serves, shoppers and merchants, and ask whether it raised its commission. Accounting would look at what the company records as revenue, and ask whether smaller discounts or a change in bookkeeping lifted the figure. National statistics would ask whether the sales of all sellers, on platforms and off them, moved with either number. Tax research would ask what the platform's records show about the sellers behind the sales. Each reading is sound, and each is partial. None of them says how large such a difference is, how often it happens, or whether it changes the story of growth.

## 2.1 Platform pricing: why revenue is only part of the commerce

Platform economics holds that a platform's revenue is a price, and that the price is set to keep two groups on board and not to collect a share of the value traded. A marketplace needs shoppers and merchants. A ride-hailing app needs riders and drivers. A shopper has little reason to visit a marketplace with no merchants, and a merchant has little reason to list on one with no shoppers, so each group is worth more to the platform when the other is present. Economists call these cross-side effects, and they are the starting point of the field (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Evans and Schmalensee 2016).

The result is that a platform does not price each group the way an ordinary seller prices a product. Because one group attracts the other, it can pay to charge one group little or nothing and to earn from the other. A delivery app might keep fees low for customers and take a larger commission from restaurants, or the reverse, depending on which group is harder to attract and which cares less about price. The split between the groups matters as well as the total, because the split decides how many people join and how many transactions follow (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Armstrong 2006). So a commission is a choice that the platform can change, and a change in it moves revenue without any change in what people buy.

The business model sets the starting point for how large the platform's share is. A marketplace earns a fee on sales between others, so its revenue is a fraction of the sales it carries. A reseller buys goods and sells them itself, so its takings are the whole sale (Hagiu and Wright 2015). Many companies mix the two, which is one reason why the share of each sale that a platform keeps differs so much from one platform to the next.

Applied to the thesis question, the theory gives a firm first answer. It says that revenue will be a small share of the commerce, that the platform's pricing sets the share, and that the platform can change it. So revenue and commerce need not move together. The theory does not say how small the share is in practice, how often platforms change it, or by how much. It is a theory of how prices are set, and it leaves the size and the steadiness of the share to measurement.

## 2.2 Accounting: what revenue records

Accounting holds that revenue is not a measure of how much was sold. It is the amount a company is entitled to record as its own income, and the rules for that decision turn on one question: is the company selling the thing itself, or arranging a sale for someone else? Suppose a ride costs the rider 100 and the driver keeps 80. A company that controls the ride, as a principal, records 100 as revenue and the driver's 80 as a cost. A company that only arranges the ride, as an agent, records its commission of 20 and never counts the driver's 80 as its own. International Financial Reporting Standard 15 (IFRS 15), the international rule on revenue, draws this line (International Accounting Standards Board 2014).

The same standard treats discounts and vouchers in a way that matters for platforms. When a company pays its customers, for example by funding a voucher, it normally treats the payment as a cut in the price. The voucher then reduces revenue and does not appear as a cost (International Accounting Standards Board 2014). So a platform that spends heavily on vouchers reports lower revenue than one that spends little on the same sales, and its revenue rises when it cuts them.

These rules leave room for judgment, and companies have used it. A study of internet firms in the early 2000s found that they chose between reporting sales gross and reporting them net, and that the choices differed across firms (Bowen, Davis and Rajgopal 2002). So the reported revenue of a platform can rise for three reasons that have nothing to do with buying: its fees rise, its discounts shrink, or it changes how it records a sale.

Companies also publish operating figures that no standard governs, and transaction value, the total value of everything bought through the platform, is one of them. Analysts read accounts more easily when the accounting is comparable across firms (De Franco, Kothari and Verdi 2011). A measure that a company chooses to report, such as transaction value or active users, loses information when the company changes its definition or stops reporting it (Givoly, Li, Lourie and Nekrasov 2019). This matters for the thesis, because transaction value is the figure against which revenue is compared.

Investors know the limits of revenue, and finance research has shown how they respond. In the early years of the internet, activity measures such as web traffic carried information about a firm's value and future revenue that reported revenue did not show (Trueman, Wong and Zhang 2000, 2001; Rajgopal, Venkatachalam and Kotha 2003).

Applied to the thesis question, accounting gives a second firm answer. Revenue is a convention, and a platform can move it by changing its commission, its discounts or its bookkeeping. The evidence does not say how large these moves are for real platforms. The studies of reporting choices describe internet firms of the early 2000s, and the studies of investors rest on web traffic and not on the transaction value of a modern platform. None of them follows the revenue and the transaction value of the same platform through several years, and none covers a market such as Indonesia.

## 2.3 National statistics: what the whole online economy counts

National statistics hold that the commerce a platform carries is not the platform's output. A country's accounts add up the goods and services it produces. They record the sale between a buyer and a seller separately from the platform's service of bringing the two together, and only the service counts as the platform's output (Ahmad and Schreyer 2016; International Monetary Fund 2018; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD] 2023; United Nations et al. 2025). The official view agrees with the pricing and accounting views: the commerce and the revenue are different amounts, and the commerce is the larger.

Counting the commerce itself is harder, because each source counts something different. Listed platforms report the revenue they record and often their transaction value. Statistics offices ask businesses about their online sales. Central banks report the value of payments. Industry reports estimate the size of whole markets from platform transaction values (Google, Temasek and Bain 2024; BPS 2025; Bank Indonesia 2025). The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) has shown that e-commerce values change with the definition used, and it has set business e-commerce sales beside the transaction values of online platforms (UNCTAD 2023, 2024).

A difference between two figures for the same activity does no harm while they move together. It matters when they part, because the choice of figure then changes the conclusion about size and growth. Economists have a standard answer to this problem: test each figure against the activity it stands for. Researchers do this when they check national income figures against satellite images of night-time lights (Henderson, Storeygard and Weil 2012).

The growth of national online sales has two possible sources. Existing sellers can sell more, or more sellers can enter. Trade economists split the growth of exports into the same two margins (Hummels and Klenow 2005). The split needs a count of sellers, and a count has weaknesses of its own. It depends on how the survey finds sellers, and a change in design can break a series in a way that looks like a change in the economy (van den Brakel, Smith and Compton 2008; van den Brakel, Zhang and Tam 2020). Respondents misdate events, and they tend to place them nearer to the interview than they were (Neter and Waksberg 1964). Small firms that stop selling are more often young firms with young owners (McKenzie and Paffhausen 2019), which matters when a count has to allow for sellers who stopped.

Applied to the thesis question, national statistics answer one part of it. They say what the whole should be measured as, and they warn that the sources will differ and that a count can break. They do not say whether platform figures and national figures grow for the same reasons, and the studies reviewed here do not test it for one country over time. They also say little about what a rise in a count of online sellers contains: more sellers, or a better count of the sellers already there.

## 2.4 Platform records and tax: seeing the commerce directly

Tax research holds that the commerce missing from a platform's revenue is not lost, because the platform sees it. A platform records every sale: who sold, what, for how much and when. A report about a taxpayer made by someone else, such as an employer, a bank or a platform, is third-party information. Tax authorities value it because it does not depend on the taxpayer's own account.

Platform records can help where other records are thin. Sellers and service providers may be small, self-employed or weakly represented in conventional financial records, yet the platform records what they sell. This differs from the shadow-economy setting, where reliable records may be sparse (La Porta and Shleifer 2014; Ulyssea 2018). Digital records can carry information that conventional records lack (Berg, Burg, Gombovic and Puri 2020), and platform-mediated work leaves traces in financial and administrative data (Barrios, Hochberg and Yi 2022; Denes, Lagaras and Tsoutsoura 2025).

Information from a third party also changes how people comply with tax. Audit experiments in Denmark, and studies of the value-added tax and of receipt lotteries, show that reports from third parties help compliance (Kleven et al. 2011; Pomeranz 2015; Naritomi 2019; Kleven, Kreiner and Saez 2016; Slemrod 2019). Tax withheld through a third party rests on the same information (Brockmeyer and Hernandez 2016).

Several rules now apply this idea to platforms. The OECD, a group of mostly high-income countries, published model rules under which platforms report what their sellers earn (OECD 2020). The European Union's DAC7 directive applies them (European Union 2021). Indonesia's marketplace tax rule goes further: it makes marketplaces collect tax from their sellers, not only report on them (Ministry of Finance of the Republic of Indonesia 2025).

Applied to the thesis question, tax research offers a way round revenue: read the records. The consensus is that platform records are useful and that rules can rest on them. The literature does not say how much of the online economy those records cover. The answer depends on how much selling happens on platforms, which is the point that national statistics left open, and the studies reviewed here do not estimate it for Indonesia.

## 2.5 The research gap

Each of the four answers is sound, and each breaks down at the same kind of point. Pricing theory and accounting explain why revenue and commerce differ, but they do not measure how far or how steadily. National statistics explain how the whole should be counted, but they do not test whether platform figures follow it. Tax research shows that records can reach the commerce, but not how much of it they reach. Three questions remain open.

1. **How far does a platform's revenue follow the commerce it carries?** The reasons for a difference are known (Sections 2.1 and 2.2). The size of the difference, and how steady it stays over several years, have not been measured for the same platforms.
2. **Do platform figures follow national growth?** Official frameworks show that the figures differ (Section 2.3). Whether platform growth tracks national growth depends on where national growth comes from, and on whether the count of sellers behind it keeps pace with entry. The studies reviewed here test neither.
3. **How far do rules built on platform records reach?** Platform records can support tax rules (Section 2.4). How much of the online economy they cover is not known.

Chapter 3 builds a framework that turns these three questions into measures and hypotheses.
