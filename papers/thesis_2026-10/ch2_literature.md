# Chapter 2. Literature Review

Platforms sit between buyers and sellers and the figures used to measure their activity. They carry transactions between buyers and sellers, record only part of the value as their own revenue, and keep records that other systems may also hold. Research explains each part of this chain from a different angle. This chapter follows the chain in four steps: how platforms earn revenue, how the online economy is measured, what platform records can show, and what remains unanswered.

## 2.1 Platform pricing and revenue recognition

Platform economics explains why the commerce a platform carries can be much larger than its revenue. Platforms connect buyers with sellers or service providers and earn commissions, fees, advertising or logistics income. They do not earn the full value of each transaction. Because a platform must attract both sides of a market, it sets prices on each side, and its revenue need not follow the commerce it coordinates (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Armstrong 2006; Evans and Schmalensee 2016). The business model matters too. A platform that connects buyers and sellers records a fee, while a reseller records the whole sale (Hagiu and Wright 2015).

Accounting rules decide how much of each sale becomes revenue. IFRS 15 is the international standard for revenue. Under it, a company that acts as a principal records the full payment from the customer, while one that acts as an agent records only what it earns from arranging the sale (International Accounting Standards Board 2014). Payments that a company makes to its own customers, such as discounts and vouchers, can reduce the revenue it reports. Studies of internet firms show that firms chose different ways of reporting revenue, including whether to report sales gross (Bowen, Davis and Rajgopal 2002). Customer incentives, service mix, acquisitions and changes in business scope can all move reported revenue without an equal change in commerce.

The usefulness of a measure also depends on how it is computed. Comparable accounting information helps analysts use financial statements (De Franco, Kothari and Verdi 2011). Operating measures that companies report themselves, such as transaction value or active users, lose information when the way they are computed changes or goes unreported (Givoly, Li, Lourie and Nekrasov 2019). Investors look past revenue as well. In the early years of the internet, measures of activity carried information about a firm's value and future revenue beyond what its reported revenue showed (Trueman, Wong and Zhang 2000, 2001; Rajgopal, Venkatachalam and Kotha 2003).

This work explains why the gap between transaction value and revenue exists and can move. It does not show how large the gap becomes in practice.

## 2.2 Measuring the online economy

Official frameworks already separate the commerce a platform carries from the service the platform provides. National accounting guidance treats the sale between buyer and seller as distinct from the platform's intermediation service. It does not count the full transaction value as the platform's output (Ahmad and Schreyer 2016; International Monetary Fund 2018; OECD 2023; United Nations et al. 2025).

In practice, different producers publish different quantities. Industry reports size the regional digital economy by transaction value (Google, Temasek and Bain 2024). Statistics offices measure the online sales of surveyed businesses (BPS 2025). Listed platforms report the revenue they recognize, and central banks report the value of payments (Bank Indonesia 2025). The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) has shown that e-commerce values change with the definition used. It has also set business e-commerce sales beside the transaction values of online platforms (UNCTAD 2023, 2024). A difference between these measures is harmless while they move together. It matters when they part, because the choice of measure can then change the conclusion about scale and growth. Research on night-time lights treats such a choice as a question to test: a measure should be checked against the activity it stands for (Henderson, Storeygard and Weil 2012).

A survey's count of sellers has a further weakness. The count depends on how the survey finds sellers, and a change in design can break a series in a way that looks like a change in the economy (van den Brakel, Smith and Compton 2008; van den Brakel, Zhang and Tam 2020). Respondents also misdate events, and they tend to place them closer to the interview than they were (Neter and Waksberg 1964). Exit among small firms is more common for young firms and young owners (McKenzie and Paffhausen 2019), which matters when a count mixes new and long-established businesses.

National online sales can grow because existing sellers sell more or because more sellers enter. Trade economists call these the intensive and extensive margins and use them to explain the growth of exports (Hummels and Klenow 2005).

## 2.3 Digital records and third-party information

Transactions that do not enter a platform's revenue still leave detailed records in its system. Sellers and service providers may be small, self-employed or weakly represented in conventional financial records, yet the platform records what they sell. This differs from the shadow-economy setting, where reliable records may be sparse (La Porta and Shleifer 2014; Ulyssea 2018). Digital records can carry information that conventional records lack (Berg, Burg, Gombovic and Puri 2020), and platform-mediated work leaves traces in financial and administrative data (Barrios, Hochberg and Yi 2022; Denes, Lagaras and Tsoutsoura 2025).

Information from a third party changes how people comply with tax. Audit experiments in Denmark, and studies of the value-added tax and of receipt lotteries, show that reports from third parties help compliance (Kleven et al. 2011; Pomeranz 2015; Naritomi 2019; Kleven, Kreiner and Saez 2016; Slemrod 2019). Tax withheld through a third party rests on the same information (Brockmeyer and Hernandez 2016).

Rules for platforms apply this idea. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), a group of mostly high-income countries, published model rules under which platforms report what their sellers earn (OECD 2020). The European Union's DAC7 directive applies them (European Union 2021). Indonesia's marketplace tax rule also makes marketplaces collect tax from their sellers, as well as report on them (Ministry of Finance of the Republic of Indonesia 2025). What a rule can show depends on the records it uses.

## 2.4 The research gap

Existing research explains the pieces of this chain separately. Platform economics explains why transaction value can exceed revenue. Accounting explains why the gap can move. Official frameworks explain why the two should not be treated as the same quantity. Research on digital records explains why some activity outside revenue stays observable in platform records. Statistical agencies and UNCTAD have begun to set business surveys beside platform transaction values.

Three questions remain open. The first is when a figure stops moving with the activity it is used to represent, and by how much. The second is whether a national count of sellers keeps pace with entry, given that survey counts can break when the way they are collected changes. The third is how far rules built on platform records reach. Answering them requires a platform's revenue and transaction value over several years, and a national survey that can be examined from the inside. Indonesia offers both. Chapter 3 turns the questions into measures and hypotheses.
