# Chapter 2. Literature Review

Digital platforms sit between economic activity and the figures used to measure it. A platform handles a transaction between a buyer and a seller, records part of the payment as its own revenue, and keeps a record of the whole sale. Each of these steps is studied by a different body of research. Platform economics and accounting explain why a platform records only part of each payment as revenue (Section 2.1). Official statistics and survey methods explain how the transactions themselves are measured across an economy (Section 2.2). Research on third-party information explains what a platform's records of each sale can show to others, such as tax authorities (Section 2.3). Each section sets out the main findings and the evidence behind them, and it closes with what the findings leave unmeasured. Section 2.4 brings these points together as the research gap.

## 2.1 Platform economics and revenue recognition

Platform economics studies firms that bring together two groups of users who benefit from each other's presence, such as buyers and sellers on a marketplace or riders and drivers on a ride-hailing service. Its central finding is that such a firm sets its prices for the two groups jointly, because the value of joining for one group depends on how many members of the other group join (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Armstrong 2006). The firm earns commissions, fees, advertising income or delivery charges for arranging transactions, and it does not take the full value of each sale (Evans and Schmalensee 2016). So the value of the commerce that passes through a platform can be much larger than the revenue the platform records.

The way a platform divides its charges between its two groups of users also matters. Rochet and Tirole (2003) show that the number of transactions on a platform depends on how the total charge is split between the two sides, and not only on its level. A platform may charge one side little or nothing and recover its costs from the other, as when a marketplace lets shoppers browse for free and charges merchants a commission on each sale (Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Armstrong 2006). Because both the level and the split are choices, a platform can change how much revenue it takes from the same volume of sales.

The business model sets how much of each sale can count as revenue in the first place. Hagiu and Wright (2015) distinguish a multi-sided platform, which lets sellers deal directly with buyers, from a reseller, which buys goods and sells them on its own account. A reseller's revenue includes the full price of what it sells. A platform's revenue is the charge it makes for the interaction, which is a fraction of the price.

Accounting rules decide how much of each payment a company records as revenue. These rules are known as revenue recognition, and International Financial Reporting Standard 15 (IFRS 15) is the international standard for them (International Accounting Standards Board 2014). The rules turn on whether the company is a principal, which controls the good or service before it reaches the customer, or an agent, which arranges for another party to provide it. A principal records the full payment as revenue, and an agent records only its fee or commission. If a rider pays 100 for a trip and the driver keeps 80, a ride-hailing company acting as an agent records 20 as revenue.

IFRS 15 also treats payments that a company makes to its own customers, such as discounts and vouchers, as a reduction of revenue, unless the payment buys a distinct good or service from the customer (International Accounting Standards Board 2014). A platform that funds vouchers for shoppers reports less revenue on the same sales, and more when it cuts the vouchers. Changes in fees, customer incentives, the mix of services, acquisitions and the scope of the reported business can all move revenue without an equal change in the commerce behind it.

Firms have used the room that revenue recognition rules leave. Bowen, Davis and Rajgopal (2002) examine how internet firms reported revenue around 2000 and find that the firms differed in whether they reported the full value of the sales they arranged, a practice known as reporting revenue gross. Comparability matters when such figures are read across firms: De Franco, Kothari and Verdi (2011) find that analysts forecast more accurately when a firm's accounting is comparable with that of its peers. Operating measures that firms disclose voluntarily, of which transaction value is one, raise a further problem. Givoly, Li, Lourie and Nekrasov (2019) find that such a measure loses information when the way it is computed changes.

Investors use operating measures alongside revenue. Trueman, Wong and Zhang (2000) find that measures of web traffic help explain the market values of internet firms beyond their financial statements, and Trueman, Wong and Zhang (2001) find that web traffic helps forecast those firms' revenue. Rajgopal, Venkatachalam and Kotha (2003) report similar results for measures of network advantage among e-commerce firms.

Platform economics and accounting research explain why a platform's revenue is smaller than the commerce it carries and why the share it keeps can change. It does not measure how large the difference is for actual platforms, or how much the share changes from one year to the next. The pricing models describe how charges are set, not how they move in a particular market. The accounting evidence comes from internet firms around 2000, and the investor evidence rests on web traffic, not on the transaction value of present-day platforms. None of the studies reviewed here follows the revenue and the transaction value of the same platforms over several years.

## 2.2 Measuring the digital economy

Official statistics distinguish the commerce that passes through a platform from the service the platform provides. National accounts, which measure a country's production and income, record a sale between a buyer and a seller as the seller's output. They record the platform's charge for arranging the sale as the platform's output, and they do not treat the full transaction value as platform output (Ahmad and Schreyer 2016; International Monetary Fund 2018; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD] 2023; United Nations et al. 2025).

In practice, different producers publish different quantities. Industry reports size the digital economy of Southeast Asia by gross merchandise value, the value of goods sold through platforms (Google, Temasek and Bain 2024). National statistics offices survey businesses about their online sales, as BPS, the Indonesian statistics office, does (BPS 2025). Listed platforms report the revenue they recognize under IFRS 15, and central banks report the value of electronic payments (Bank Indonesia 2025). The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) documents how far e-commerce values depend on the definition used, and it compares business e-commerce sales with the transaction values of large online platforms (UNCTAD 2023, 2024).

A difference between two figures for the same activity is harmless while they move together. It matters when they part, because the choice of figure can then change the conclusion about size and growth. Research on night-time lights treats this as a question to be tested. Henderson, Storeygard and Weil (2012) compare national income figures with satellite measures of light at night and use the relationship to improve estimates of growth where official figures are weak.

National online sales can grow because existing sellers sell more or because more sellers enter. Trade economists make the same distinction for exports. Hummels and Klenow (2005) divide differences in exports between countries into an intensive margin, more of the same goods, and an extensive margin, a wider range of goods. The same split applied to online sales separates growth that comes from more sellers from growth that comes from higher sales per seller.

A count of sellers comes from a survey, and surveys change. Van den Brakel, Smith and Compton (2008) show that a change in survey design can produce a break in a series that looks like a real change, and van den Brakel, Zhang and Tam (2020) review methods for measuring such breaks. Respondents also misdate past events. In a study of household spending, Neter and Waksberg (1964) find that people tend to report events as more recent than they were. Firm exit matters as well: McKenzie and Paffhausen (2019) find that small firms in developing countries that stop operating are more often young firms with young owners.

Official statistics and survey research establish that platform transaction value and platform revenue measure different things, and that the figures used to describe online commerce come from different sources with different definitions. It does not test whether platform figures and national figures grow for the same reasons. It also offers no test of what a rise in a national count of online sellers contains: sellers who are new, or sellers whom the survey had not reached before.

## 2.3 Digital records and third-party information

Transactions that do not enter a platform's revenue are still recorded in its systems. A platform stores who sold what, to whom, for how much and when. Many of the sellers and service providers involved are small, self-employed or weakly represented in conventional financial records. This differs from the setting of the shadow economy, where reliable records of activity are often missing (La Porta and Shleifer 2014; Ulyssea 2018). Digital records can carry information that conventional records lack. Berg, Burg, Gombovic and Puri (2020) find that simple digital traces, such as the type of device a customer uses, help predict loan default. Activity on platforms also leaves traces in financial and administrative data (Barrios, Hochberg and Yi 2022; Denes, Lagaras and Tsoutsoura 2025).

Information that a third party reports about a taxpayer improves tax compliance. In a field experiment in Denmark, Kleven et al. (2011) find that evasion is close to zero for income that third parties such as employers report, and substantial for income that taxpayers report themselves. Pomeranz (2015) shows that the paper trail created by the value-added tax in Chile raises compliance. Naritomi (2019) finds that rewarding consumers in Brazil for asking for receipts raised the sales that firms reported. Kleven, Kreiner and Saez (2016) and Slemrod (2019) review this evidence, and Brockmeyer and Hernandez (2016) study tax withheld by third parties in Costa Rica.

Rules for platforms now apply the principle of third-party reporting. The OECD, a group of mostly high-income countries, published model rules under which platforms report what their sellers earn (OECD 2020). The European Union's seventh Directive on Administrative Cooperation, known as DAC7, applies these rules in its member states (European Union 2021). Indonesia's rule for marketplaces goes further: it requires marketplaces to identify their sellers, report their transactions and collect tax from them (Ministry of Finance of the Republic of Indonesia 2025).

Research on digital records and third-party information shows that platform records contain information that tax authorities can use, and that rules now oblige platforms to report it or to collect tax on it. It does not measure how much of a country's online selling those records cover. Where many sellers trade outside platforms, a rule built on platform records reaches only part of them, and the studies reviewed here do not estimate that part for Indonesia.

## 2.4 Research gap

Existing research explains the parts of the problem separately. Platform economics explains why transaction value can exceed revenue, and accounting explains why the share recorded as revenue can move. Official statistics explain why the two quantities should not be treated as the same, and survey methods explain why a count of sellers can break. Research on third-party information explains why activity outside revenue can still be observed in platform records. Three questions remain open, and together they make up the research gap.

1. **How large is the difference between transaction value and revenue in practice?** No study reviewed here measures it for the same platforms in a market such as Indonesia.
2. **Does the difference persist, or does it move over time?** The share a platform keeps can change, but no study reviewed here follows it across several years.
3. **Does the choice of figure change the conclusion about growth?** Platform, national and payment figures differ. No study reviewed here tests whether they grow for the same reasons, or what a rise in the count of sellers behind national growth contains.

The thesis addresses these questions by measuring the invisible wedge, the difference between transaction value and revenue, over time. It traces the disclosed reasons for the largest movements and compares platform figures with national and payment figures. A further question follows from Section 2.3 and extends the thesis beyond measurement: how far rules built on platform records can reach. The thesis addresses it by estimating how many online sellers such rules can reach.
