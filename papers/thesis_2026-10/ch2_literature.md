# Chapter 2. Literature Review

The question of how much of an online economy a platform's revenue leaves out, and when that matters for growth, has not been answered in one place. Four bodies of research have each answered part of it, from different directions. Platform economics explains why a platform keeps only a share of what its users trade. Accounting explains what a company may record as revenue. National statistics explain how the commerce of a country is counted. Tax research explains what the records of a platform can be used for.

A case from Indonesia shows what the four fields can and cannot do. In 2023 Tokopedia's revenue rose by 53 percent while the value of the sales it carried fell by 9 percent. Platform economics would look for a change in how the platform prices the two groups it serves, shoppers and merchants. Accounting would look for a change in what the company records as revenue, such as smaller discounts. National statistics would ask whether the sales of all sellers, on platforms and off them, moved with either figure. Tax research would ask what the platform's records show about the sellers behind the sales. Each reading is sound. None says how large such a difference is, how often it occurs, or whether it changes the story of growth.

This chapter takes the four fields in turn. For each, it sets out what the field has established, what answer that gives to the thesis question, and where the answer stops. Section 2.4 gathers the stopping points into the gap that the thesis addresses.

## 2.1 Why revenue differs from commerce: pricing and accounting

Economic theory treats a platform's revenue as the price of an introduction and not as the value of what is traded. A platform brings two groups together: buyers and sellers on a marketplace, riders and drivers on a ride-hailing app. Each group joins mainly because the other is already there, so the platform has to attract both before it can earn from either (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Caillaud and Jullien 2003; Evans and Schmalensee 2016). This is the central result of the field, and it fits a platform whose revenue is a small share of the commerce that passes through it.

Attracting two groups also shapes the price. A platform may charge one group little or nothing and earn from the other, because the presence of the free group makes the platform worth more to the paying group. The total fee and the way it is split between the groups are both choices, and the split affects how many transactions take place (Rochet and Tirole 2003; Parker and Van Alstyne 2005; Armstrong 2006). So a platform can change how much it earns from a given volume of commerce by changing its prices. The business model sets the starting point. A marketplace earns a fee on each sale between others, while a reseller buys goods and sells them itself (Hagiu and Wright 2015). The fee is a fraction of the sale, and the reseller's takings are the whole sale.

Accounting rules add a second reason for the difference. They decide how much of a sale a company may record as its own revenue, and the international rule is International Financial Reporting Standard 15 (IFRS 15). It asks whether a company acts as a principal or as an agent. A principal controls the goods or service before they reach the customer and records the whole price as revenue. An agent only arranges the sale and records its commission. The standard also normally treats discounts and vouchers that a company gives its customers as a cut in the price, so they reduce its revenue (International Accounting Standards Board 2014).

Companies have some room to choose how they record revenue. A study of internet firms in the early 2000s found that they reported the same kind of sale in different ways, including whether to report it gross (Bowen, Davis and Rajgopal 2002). For a platform, then, reported revenue can rise because fees rise, because discounts shrink, or because the company changes how it records a sale. None of the three needs a change in what customers buy.

Companies also report operating measures of their own, such as transaction value or active users. Analysts use financial statements more easily when the accounting is comparable (De Franco, Kothari and Verdi 2011). Operating measures lose information when the company changes its definition or stops reporting them (Givoly, Li, Lourie and Nekrasov 2019). Transaction value, the figure on which this thesis relies, is such a measure.

Investors do not rely on revenue alone. In the early years of the internet, activity measures such as web traffic carried information about a firm's value and future revenue that reported revenue did not show (Trueman, Wong and Zhang 2000, 2001; Rajgopal, Venkatachalam and Kotha 2003).

Pricing theory and accounting together answer why revenue and commerce differ, and the answer fits a case like Tokopedia's. They do not answer how much. The theory explains how prices are set, but it does not say how large a platform's share should be or how steady it should stay. The accounting evidence concerns the rules and the reporting choices of internet firms in the early 2000s. The investor evidence rests on web traffic, which is not the transaction value of a modern platform. None of it follows the revenue and the transaction value of the same platforms through several years, and none covers a market such as Indonesia.

## 2.2 How the online economy is counted

Official statistics give a firm answer on what a platform's commerce is not: it is not the platform's own output. National accounts add up a country's production, income and spending. They treat the sale between a buyer and a seller as one thing and the platform's service of bringing them together as another. So the full transaction value does not count as the platform's output (Ahmad and Schreyer 2016; International Monetary Fund 2018; Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development [OECD] 2023; United Nations et al. 2025).

Counting the commerce itself is harder, because each producer of figures counts something different. Listed platforms report the revenue they record and often their transaction value. Statistics offices survey businesses about their online sales. Central banks report the value of payments. Industry reports estimate the size of whole markets from platform transaction values (Google, Temasek and Bain 2024; BPS 2025; Bank Indonesia 2025). The United Nations Conference on Trade and Development (UNCTAD) has shown that e-commerce values change with the definition used, and it has set business e-commerce sales beside the transaction values of online platforms (UNCTAD 2023, 2024).

A difference between two figures for the same activity does no harm while they move together. It matters when they part, because the choice of figure then changes the conclusion about size and growth. Economists have a method for this problem. They test a figure against the activity it stands for, as researchers do when they check national income figures against satellite images of night-time lights (Henderson, Storeygard and Weil 2012).

The growth of national online sales can be tested in the same spirit, because it has two possible sources. Existing sellers can sell more, or more sellers can enter. Trade economists divide the growth of exports into the same two kinds of margin (Hummels and Klenow 2005).

The split of national growth rests on a count of online sellers, and that count has a weakness of its own. It depends on how the survey finds sellers. A change in design can break a series in a way that looks like a change in the economy (van den Brakel, Smith and Compton 2008; van den Brakel, Zhang and Tam 2020). Respondents also misdate events, and they tend to place them closer to the interview than they were (Neter and Waksberg 1964). Exit among small firms is more common for young firms and young owners (McKenzie and Paffhausen 2019), which matters when a count has to be corrected for sellers who stopped.

Official frameworks and survey methods say what should be counted and how the figures can mislead. They do not say whether platform figures and national figures grow for the same reasons, and the studies reviewed here do not test it for one country over time. They also say little about what a rise in a count of online sellers contains.

## 2.3 What platform records can show

Tax research offers a different answer to the question. If revenue shows little of the commerce, the commerce can still be seen in the platform's records, because a platform records every sale: who sold, what, for how much and when. A report about a taxpayer made by someone else, such as an employer, a bank or a platform, is third-party information. Tax authorities value it because it does not depend on the taxpayer's own account.

Platform records can help where other records are thin. Sellers and service providers may be small, self-employed or weakly represented in conventional financial records, yet the platform records what they sell. This differs from the shadow-economy setting, where reliable records may be sparse (La Porta and Shleifer 2014; Ulyssea 2018). Digital records can carry information that conventional records lack (Berg, Burg, Gombovic and Puri 2020), and platform-mediated work leaves traces in financial and administrative data (Barrios, Hochberg and Yi 2022; Denes, Lagaras and Tsoutsoura 2025).

Information from a third party also changes how people comply with tax. Audit experiments in Denmark, and studies of the value-added tax and of receipt lotteries, show that reports from third parties help compliance (Kleven et al. 2011; Pomeranz 2015; Naritomi 2019; Kleven, Kreiner and Saez 2016; Slemrod 2019). Tax withheld through a third party rests on the same information (Brockmeyer and Hernandez 2016).

Several rules now apply third-party reporting to platforms. The OECD, a group of mostly high-income countries, published model rules under which platforms report what their sellers earn (OECD 2020). The European Union's DAC7 directive applies them (European Union 2021). Indonesia's marketplace tax rule goes further: it makes marketplaces collect tax from their sellers, not only report on them (Ministry of Finance of the Republic of Indonesia 2025).

The consensus on platform records is that they are useful and that rules can rest on them. The literature does not say how many of a country's online sellers such a rule can reach. The answer depends on how much of the selling happens on platforms, which is the very point that Section 2.2 left open. The studies reviewed here do not estimate it for Indonesia.

## 2.4 The research gap

Read together, the four answers stop at the same place. Each explains why figures can differ, and none measures how far they differ, how steadily, or with what effect on growth. Three questions remain open.

1. **How far does a platform's revenue follow the commerce it carries?** Pricing theory and accounting give reasons why the two part (Section 2.1). No study reviewed here follows the revenue and the transaction value of the same platforms over several years to measure how much revenue leaves out and how steady that share is.
2. **Do platform figures follow national growth?** Official frameworks show that figures differ (Section 2.2). Whether platform growth tracks national growth depends on where national growth comes from and on whether the count of sellers behind it keeps pace with entry. The studies reviewed here test neither.
3. **How far do rules built on platform records reach?** Tax research shows that platform records can support rules (Section 2.3). It does not show how much of the online economy those records cover.

Chapter 3 builds the framework that turns these questions into measures and hypotheses.
