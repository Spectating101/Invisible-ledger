# Chapter 6. The Rules and How Far They Reach

Indonesia's government is writing rules that act on the figures this thesis measures. In 2026 it capped a ride-hailing commission and limited discounts on marketplaces. It has also set a tax that marketplaces will collect from their sellers and required platforms to send data to BPS, and BPS has run a new economic census. Each rule acts on one part of what Chapter 5 measured, and each reaches only that part.

The marketplace tax is the rule aimed most directly at online sellers. It can collect from at most about 1 in 20 online sellers and about a fifth of online sales value. It also gives the state a record of every marketplace seller, including those who pay nothing. This chapter measures what each rule can reach, using the data of Chapter 5. What each rule changes can be seen only after it takes effect, and Section 6.5 sets out the predictions that will be scored on those results.

## 6.1 The rules and what each acts on

The recent rules act on three parts of the figures: the platforms' take rate, the sales of sellers inside marketplaces, and BPS's coverage. Table 6.1 lists each rule with the part it acts on and the section of Chapter 5 that measures that part.

| Discount rule (Trade Ministry Regulation 19/2026, Art. 18) | Bars marketplaces from repeated price subsidies and discounts below fair cost | The incentive rate | 5.3 | Primary text read |

Equation (6) shows where the first two rules act. The take rate is the fee rate less the incentive rate. A cap on a fee pushes the take rate down. A limit on incentives pushes it up. The two rules act on opposite sides of the same take rate.

The marketplace tax acts further out, on the sales of sellers inside marketplaces. It leaves the take rate alone, but it depends on the marketplaces' records of each sale. The platform-data rule and the census act on the national figure: the first on what BPS receives, the second on where BPS looks.

Two rules serve as context. The first is the 2023 ban on transactions on social media platforms, the step with which Chapter 1 began. Selling through chat and social media remained the norm, and in 2023 about 96 percent of online sellers used them. The second is the small-business tax, which applies in every channel. The survey files cannot identify the content creators it excludes, so the thesis does not measure its reach.

The sections that follow take the other rules in the order of Figure 3.1, from the take rate outward.

## 6.2 What the commission cap and the discount rule can move

The commission cap reaches a small part of the take rates this thesis measures. It followed protests by ride-hailing drivers, who had demanded a smaller cut since 2025 (Pasardana 2025). From July 2026, a platform may keep at most 8 percent of the fare on a two-wheel ride. Car rides, food delivery and marketplaces are not covered.

The trips under the cap are a small share of each company's business. At GoTo, the two-wheel ride service brings about 7 percent of group net revenue (GoTo Gojek Tokopedia 2026). Across Grab's whole region, rides are about 36 percent of on-demand transaction value, and two-wheel trips in Indonesia are less than 6 percent of its rides business. [Source of the 6 percent to be confirmed before this sentence is kept.] Both companies report rides and deliveries as one on-demand take rate, so the cap acts on a slice of the figure Chapter 5 measured.

A cap on one fee does not fix the take rate, because the platform still sets its incentives. At GoTo, on-demand revenue rose by about 20 percent in the second quarter of 2026 while transaction value grew by 2 percent. The company attributed the rise to its mix of services and to "rationalization of incentives as we prepared for the implementation of the 8% commission rate" (GoTo Gojek Tokopedia 2026). Its take rate rose to about 22 percent in that quarter, the last before the cap.

The discount rule acts on the lever that moved take rates most often. Lower incentives were the larger part of the rise in the take rate in four of the seven windows of Table 5.4. The rule covers marketplaces and other online trade.

Where incentives can be followed over time, much of that lever had been pulled before the rule came. Tokopedia, the only marketplace that reported its incentives, cut them by about a third in 2023, its last full year of separate reporting. At GoTo's on-demand business, incentives fell from about 11 percent of transaction value in 2023 to about 4 percent in 2025. Grab still spent about 10 percent of on-demand transaction value on incentives in the four quarters to early 2026, across the region. The thesis did not register a test of the discount rule, because its effect on the take rate cannot be told apart from changes in fees.

Marketplace fees moved the other way. Shopee raised the fees it charges sellers through 2025, and Shopee and TikTok Shop with Tokopedia raised them again in 2026 (Katadata 2025; CNN Indonesia 2026). The government declined to cap them. The rules on the take rate cap one fee on one ride-hailing service and leave the largest marketplace fees free. How far the cap moves the companies' take rates is the subject of the predictions in Section 6.5.

## 6.3 How far the marketplace tax reaches

The marketplace tax can collect from few online sellers, but it gives the state a record of every marketplace seller. Marketplaces will collect 0.5 percent of each domestic seller's gross sales and pass it to the tax office. Individual sellers with sales up to about US$34,000 a year are exempt (Section 4.8). Collection is due to start in November 2026, after the start date has already moved twice.

At most about 1 in 20 online sellers falls under the tax. In 2022, marketplace sellers with sales above BPS's bracket line of about US$20,000 a year were about 5 percent of all online sellers. The exemption sits higher, at about US$34,000, so the true share is smaller.

The value the tax reaches is larger, but it is still a minority of online sales. Marketplaces carried 23 to 26 percent of online sales value in 2022, and sellers above the bracket line held 77 to 94 percent of that. So the tax reaches at most 17 to 24 percent of online sales value, about a fifth.

The record reaches further than the tax. Every domestic seller on a designated marketplace must give the marketplace a tax number or national ID and an address before being paid. Exempt sellers file a statement as well. Marketplace sellers were about 18 percent of online sellers in 2023, so the tax gives the state a record of nearly one seller in five, including the many who pay nothing.

The tax leaves the other four in five sellers where they were. Sellers off the marketplaces sell through chat, social media or their own websites, and they keep complete financial statements less often (Section 5.9). Few of them plan to move. In the survey for 2023, about 13 percent of sellers off the marketplaces wanted to join one.

The revenue at stake is small. At 0.5 percent, the tax would raise about US$60 million a year on BPS's marketplace sales for 2024, before exemptions. On e-Conomy's estimate of e-commerce transaction value, about five times larger, it would raise about US$300 million. Even the larger amount is about 0.2 percent of the government's tax target for 2026.

The tax adds a record more than revenue. Whether collection starts in November, and whether sellers move away from marketplaces once it does, will be known only after the start.

## 6.4 What the statistics rules can change

The rules on statistics act on the outer link, where the national figures parted in 2023. Since 2024, e-commerce operators must send BPS their revenue, transactions and numbers of sellers and buyers. Operators that do not comply face sanctions. By late 2024, 61 operators had sent data and 75 registered operators had not (Republika 2024). BPS's publication for 2024 describes its figures as survey-based and does not mention the platform data.

Full compliance would still leave most online selling to the survey. Platform data describe the marketplace slice, which holds about a fifth of online sales value and under a fifth of sellers (Section 5.9). The chat and social sellers, where the national figures part (Section 5.10), would still be counted only by BPS's survey.

The economic census of 2026 acts on where the survey looks. BPS's e-commerce survey draws its areas from the economic census of 2016. The 2026 census visited businesses door to door from June to August 2026 and counted online sellers, affiliates and influencers for the first time. Its results had not been released when this thesis was written.

A new frame can move the count of sellers without any change in selling. About half of the 2023 rise was not explained by first-time entry (Section 5.7). When BPS adopts a frame from the 2026 census, counts on either side of the change will need the same care. The entry test of Section 3.5 can be repeated on the first survey that uses the new frame.

## 6.6 Beyond Indonesia

Other countries are building rules on platform records too, and the same question of reach applies to them. The OECD's model rules ask platforms to report what their sellers earn, and a later module added the sale of goods (OECD 2020). The European Union applies such rules through its DAC7 directive, with reports on activity from 2023 (European Union 2021). The Philippines has platforms withhold tax from sellers' payouts, with small sellers exempt (Bureau of Internal Revenue 2023). Viet Nam has had platforms with a payment function withhold tax for household sellers since 2025 (Government of Viet Nam 2025).

Indonesia's design goes further than reporting, because its marketplaces collect the tax as well as identify the seller. Every one of these rules reaches only the sales that pass through a platform. Where most selling runs through platforms, a rule built on their records reaches most sellers. Where it runs through chat and social media, as in Indonesia, it reaches a minority.

Commission caps have been tried elsewhere as well. Several US cities and states capped the commissions that delivery platforms charged restaurants. The platforms raised the delivery fees they charged consumers, and independent restaurants lost orders while chains gained (Li and Wang 2025). In the terms of equation (6), a cap on one fee left the platform free to change another. Indonesia's cap also covers one fee on one service.

The thesis does not measure the split between channels outside Indonesia. Any statistics office that asks sellers which channels they use can make the same count, and with it measure how far its own rules reach.
