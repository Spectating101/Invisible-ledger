# Chapter 5. Results

This chapter reports the results in the order of the thesis. It starts with the platforms, then moves to the national figures. Each result is marked registered, exploratory or descriptive. Section 4.8 defines the first two. A descriptive result reports numbers without a test.

## 5.1 How much revenue leaves out

In 2023, three major platforms in Indonesia carried US$43.2 billion of transaction value between them. They kept US$3.2 billion as revenue. The sum of their invisible wedges was US$40.1 billion. Revenue was about 7 percent of what passed through them. Their ecosystem ratio, the wedge divided by revenue, was 12.7. The ratio is the author's own measure. These results are descriptive. Table 5.1 shows the three platforms and how each amount was obtained.

**Table 5.1. The invisible wedge of three platforms, 2023 (US$ billion)**

| Platform | Transaction value | Revenue | Wedge | How it is labelled |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tokopedia | 16.33 | 0.41 | 15.93 | Both direct segment disclosure |
| Shopee, Indonesia | 21.52 | 2.15 | 19.37 | Transaction value is an outside estimate (Momentum Works); revenue is derived at an assumed take rate of 10 percent |
| Grab, Indonesia | 5.38 | 0.61 | 4.78 | Revenue is a direct country disclosure; transaction value is derived from Grab's company-wide ratio |
| Total | 43.23 | 3.16 | 40.07 | Rounded amounts may not add |

The size of the wedge changes little with the assumptions. Assume instead that Shopee keeps 9 or 11 percent, and the total wedge stays between US$39.9 billion and US$40.3 billion. At Sea's company-wide rate of 11.5 percent in 2023, it would be US$39.8 billion. Drop any one platform, and the wedge is still at least US$20.7 billion. For the platforms that report both amounts directly, the wedge is 97 to 99 percent of transaction value in every year. It moves almost one for one with transaction value. The appendix gives the wedge for each platform and each year available between 2020 and 2025.

The platforms are only part of online selling. In the BPS survey, the marketplace channel carries about a fifth of online sales value. The share is between 16 and 26 percent, depending on the year and the scenario. BPS counts food and ride apps in this channel. So a measure built on platforms misses between about 74 and 84 percent of online sales value. Revenue, which is about 7 percent of what the platforms carry, shows much less still.

## 5.2 Do revenue and transaction value grow together?

H1 is rejected. Among Indonesian and regional platforms, revenue did not grow in proportion to transaction value. Table 5.2 gives the tests, which compare five such platforms with 27 foreign ones. Three are Indonesian marketplaces, and two are the on-demand segments of Grab and GoTo, which cover more than Indonesia. The typical platform in the Indonesian and regional group changed its take rate by about 20 percent in a year, up or down. The typical foreign platform changed it by about 6 percent. The difference is about threefold. The first version of the test used only the three marketplaces. The larger groups were set up after that result was known, so the main test is not registered.

**Table 5.2. The test of H1**

| Test | Method | Indonesian and regional group | Foreign group | p-value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yearly change in log take rate, 5 platforms against 27 | Mann–Whitney | 0.20 | 0.06 | 0.004 |
| The same | Shuffle | 0.20 | 0.06 | 0.022 |
| Growth divergence in points, 5 platforms against 26 | Mann–Whitney | 27.0 | 6.5 | 0.005 |
| Drop each Indonesian platform in turn | Mann–Whitney | 0.17 to 0.23 | 0.06 | 0.003 to 0.016 |
| Tokopedia and GoTo on-demand as one company, 4 against 27 | Mann–Whitney | — | — | 0.003 |
| Three marketplaces against eight foreign platforms, growth divergence | Shuffle | 38.9 | 7.1 | 0.06 |
| The same, without Sea | Shuffle | 38.9 | 4.2 | 0.008 |
| Quarterly pairs, 3 platforms against 8 | Mann–Whitney | 0.071 | 0.049 | 0.19 |

The first two rows are the main test, with the two methods of Chapter 4. In rows one to five, the group figures are medians of platform medians. The third row repeats the test with growth divergence in points, as Chapter 3 defines it. Ozon has no pair in which both years pass the own-goods rule, so that row has 26 foreign platforms. The dropping and grouping rows show that no single platform carries the result. The two marketplace rows use pair-level medians. This narrower test gives 0.06 on growth divergence and 0.03 on the take-rate change. It uses a smaller benchmark and a different measure, so it is not a stricter form of the main test. Removing Sea from its benchmark lowers these p-values to 0.008 and 0.017. The quarterly test was registered before it was run, but after the annual result was known.

A second feature separates the groups. In one third of Indonesian pairs, the take rate stayed within about 10 percent. Abroad, it did so in about two thirds. The quarterly version of the test does not reject H1 (Table 5.2). Its Indonesian pairs mostly cover 2023 to 2026, after most of the repricing. The large moves came in 2022 to 2024, so the rejection of H1 describes that period.

Most of the threefold difference comes from the size of the slice. This part is exploratory. Measured in percentage points of the take rate, Indonesian and regional platforms changed it by about as much as foreign ones. The change was about 1 point a year in both groups, with a p-value of 0.65. But the typical Indonesian and regional platform keeps only 1.6 percent of each sale, against 16.9 percent abroad. If transaction value is flat, a rise of 1 point lifts the revenue of the first by more than half. The same rise lifts the revenue of a typical foreign platform by about 6 percent. The larger the wedge is next to revenue, the more a small price change moves revenue growth.

## 5.3 Why the take rate moved

Revenue moved mainly because platforms changed what they keep. Two results show this. First, the thesis divides each movement in revenue into volume and take rate, as section 4.3 describes. For the three Indonesian marketplaces, the take rate accounts for two thirds of the movement at the median (66.5 percent). For the eight platforms of the first foreign benchmark, it accounts for about a quarter (26.7 percent). The wedge follows the volume part. At the median, about 96 percent of a change in the wedge comes from the change in transaction value, in both groups. These results are descriptive.

Second, Table 5.3 splits the growth of revenue into three parts. They are more commerce, higher fees and lower incentives, which are the discounts and vouchers a platform pays for. Fees and incentives are measured per unit of transaction value. A negative share means that the part pulled revenue down. Windows of one platform overlap, so the seven windows are not seven independent cases.

**Table 5.3. What moved revenue in seven Indonesian windows (share of the growth of revenue, percent)**

| Platform and window | Growth of revenue | More commerce | Higher fees | Lower incentives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tokopedia, 2022–23 | +53% | −22 | 77 | 45 |
| Blibli third-party retail, 2022–23 | +465% | 17 | 20 | 63 |
| Blibli third-party retail, 2022–25 | +679% | 18 | 63 | 20 |
| Grab on-demand, 2021–24 | +321% | 34 | 34 | 33 |
| Grab on-demand, 2022–24 | +86% | 43 | 11 | 47 |
| GoTo on-demand, 2022–25 | +111% | 12 | 36 | 52 |
| GoTo on-demand, 2023–25 | +114% | 27 | 6 | 67 |

Table 5.3 gives rounded shares, so a row can add to 101. Lower incentives were the larger of the two take-rate parts in four windows, and higher fees in two. In the seventh, Grab from 2021 to 2024, the two were nearly equal (34 and 33 percent). Grab's figures come from its annual filings, and Tokopedia's and GoTo's from audited statements. GoTo's 2022 transaction value is an estimate. Blibli does not report incentives, so its split rests on a proxy. Blibli's releases credit part of its 2022–23 rise to travel and digital products inside the segment. So some of its fee and incentive shares reflect a change in mix, which the reports do not let us separate. For Bukalapak, the reports do not allow the parts to be separated.

Tokopedia needs two views. Its accounts deduct incentives from revenue, and they fell by about one third in 2023. In the same year its transaction value fell by 9 percent and its revenue rose by 53 percent. Counting the lines of its accounts, 60 percent of the extra net revenue came from lower incentives and 40 percent from higher gross fees. That count leaves the fall in volume inside both lines. Table 5.3 takes it out and compares fees and incentives per unit of transaction value. On that basis, higher fees account for more of the rise in the take rate than lower incentives. Both views agree that pricing, and not more commerce, drove the rise. They differ on which price lever led.

On Grab's quarterly figures, its Southeast Asian on-demand take rate rose by 3.9 points in 2022–23. Of these, 3.5 came from pricing within rides and deliveries. Across the windows, revenue in 2022 to 2024 was not a fair guide to the commerce these platforms carried. The take rate moved a lot, mostly through lower incentives and higher fees.

## 5.4 What markets valued

The market-value results are registered and one-sided. One prediction was that market value moves with transaction value. It held. Across 28 listed platforms and 138 platform-years, a 1 percent rise in transaction value goes with a 0.6 percent rise in market value. The p-value is 0.002. The other prediction was that markets value growth from a higher take rate less than growth in transaction value. It did not hold. A 1 percent rise in the take rate goes with a rise of 0.3 percent. Its standard error is 0.37, too wide to tell it from zero. The difference between the two is not large enough to count (p = 0.20). The difference counts only when segment series and three more whole companies are added, and the thesis does not rely on that run. Both results are associations. Market value also changes when a company issues shares, so neither is a stock return. Market value thus moved with the buying underneath revenue, and the next section asks what that buying did in 2023.

These results are descriptive. Buying through platforms did not boom in 2023. Table 5.4 sets the Indonesian measures against household spending, which rose by 9.4 percent in current prices. Of six measures of buying through Indonesian platforms, five grew more slowly, and three fell. Blibli is the exception.

## 5.6 What sellers reported

Sellers did not describe a boom in 2023. BPS asks each business how its online sales changed. Among businesses already selling, the median change was zero, as a registered prediction expected. The other figures in this section are descriptive. A share of 24 percent reported a rise, 32 percent a fall and 44 percent no change. In 2022, the balance was the other way: 37 percent reported a rise and 24 percent a fall. The mean reported change in 2023 was −4.3 percent. Lack of demand became the main obstacle, named by 41 percent of sellers in 2023 and 35 percent in 2022. Yet the published count of online sellers rose by 27 percent in the same year. Section 5.7 asks where that rise came from.

## 5.7 The count of online sellers and the entry test

On BPS's published figures, H2 holds. More businesses account for 71 percent of the growth in online sales value from 2022 to 2023. They account for 90 percent from 2023 to 2024, or 71 percent if BPS's other count for 2023 is used. The split is arithmetic, and it cannot tell a new seller from one counted for the first time.

The entry test can. This result is registered. In the 2024 file, 13.45 percent of sellers began selling online in 2023. The rule required at least 21.5 percent. The entry test fails. Even if no seller stopped, 13.45 percent new sellers means the count could have grown by at most 15.5 percent over the sellers already there. The published growth is 27.4 percent.

**Table 5.5. The 2023 rise in the count of online sellers (thousands)**

| Part of the rise | If no seller stopped | If sellers stopped at 5 percent a year |
|---|---|---|
| Rise between the weighted totals of the two files | 819 (100%) | 819 (100%) |
| Net entry: sellers who began in 2023, less those who stopped | 513 (63%) | 360 (44%) |
| Earlier sellers newly counted above the whole 2022 count | 306 (37%) | 458 (56%) |

Table 5.5 shows the accounting. The rise of 819 thousand is the gap between the weighted totals of the two files, and BPS's own published counts give 821 thousand. The 2023 survey counted 306 thousand more sellers who began before 2023 than the whole 2022 count held. This is 37 percent of the rise if no seller stopped. If sellers stopped at 5 percent a year, it is 56 percent. So about half of the rise is not explained by first-time entry. This accounting is exploratory, like the cohort check and the four further checks, and they point the same way.

The cohort check compares groups of sellers across surveys. Sellers who began selling by 2020 numbered 9.9 percent fewer in the 2022 survey than in the 2020 survey, as a cohort should. That fall took two years, or about 5 percent a year. In the 2023 survey they numbered 6.0 percent more than in the 2022 one. From 2023 to 2024, the cohorts shrink again. Sellers who began before 2020 fell by 10.2 percent in that one year. At least 754 thousand new sellers appeared, against a rise of 584 thousand. So 2023 stands out as the break.

The four further checks ask where the break sits and how large it is. The first compares the 34 provinces. A rank correlation of 1 means that two measures rank the provinces alike, and 0 means no link. Count growth had almost no link to the share of new sellers (0.10, p = 0.59). It had a weak link to growth in sample size (0.35, p = 0.04). In 23 provinces, growth exceeded what entry can explain, and these carry 98.5 percent of the increase. The second check splits sellers by channel. Among sellers who began by 2022, those using only chat or social media rose by 13 percent, and marketplace users rose by 0.3 percent.

The third check asks about sampling error. BPS's published error rates imply a standard error of about 1.4 percent for the national count. This is the size of error that sampling alone would typically cause. The 306 thousand sellers are about 4.6 times the standard error of the change. They would be 2.3 times it if the true error were twice as large. The fourth check is the sellers' own reports in section 5.6, which do not describe a rise in sales.

The value side tells a similar story. BPS's value rose from about US$53 billion to about US$72 billion. Sellers not explained by entry account for 15 to 17 percent of this, and entrants for 25 to 30 percent. The remaining 53 to 59 percent cannot be traced to existing sellers' answers, which show a mean change of −4.3 percent. The channel and province checks point to wider survey coverage of chat and social sellers. The data cannot separate that from sellers returning after a pause. Section 5.8 asks who the extra sellers were, and section 5.9 asks whether they returned.

## 5.8 Who the extra sellers were

The comparison of who the extra sellers were is exploratory. The surveys do not follow the same businesses, so the thesis compares groups. For each group, it takes the 2023 count above what the group's normal yearly change in 2020 to 2022 would give. These extra sellers total about 450 thousand, close to the 458 thousand of the second column in Table 5.5. Table 5.6 gives the share of this extra count held by each group, next to the group's share of the 2022 count.

**Table 5.6. Who the extra sellers were (percent)**

| Group | Share of the 2022 count | Share of the extra count |
|---|---|---|
| Business opened before 2010 | 18.3 | 40.5 |
| Owner aged 50 or over | 28.9 | 41.2 |
| Outside Java | 23.6 | 36.3 |
| Food and lodging, or making goods | 38.5 | 53.0 |

The extra count leaned toward long-running businesses, older owners and places outside Java. The lean toward men in the raw data disappears once normal change is taken out. The extra count does not look like the young start-ups of the usual growth story. It raises one question: could these be established sellers who paused and came back?

## 5.9 Could they be sellers who returned?

A seller returning from a break would usually sell for only part of the year, unless the seller restarts in January. The exploratory check looks at part-year selling, which fell in 2023. BPS changed how it asks about months between the two rounds. Among sellers who began by 2021, the number selling for part of the year fell from 472 thousand to 318 thousand. It fell in every start-year group. Small firms that stop are more often young, with young owners (McKenzie and Paffhausen 2019), and sellers who pause are likely to be such firms. If returners were mostly such firms, the extra count would lean young. It leaned old. Returners equal to 10 to 15 percent of the 2022 count would remove the unexplained part. This is a scenario, not an estimate. The check cannot rule out returners: the files carry no linked histories, and exits or a move to full-year selling could offset them.

## 5.10 Channels and records

Two additional analyses, on channels and on records, are reported here. The path of marketplace use, the value held by sellers without statements and the World Bank results were registered. The comparison within the pre-2020 group is exploratory. The value shares come from bracket scenarios. The annual reading of the brackets passes the validation rule, because the middle scenario gives 1.95 times BPS's total for 2022. The monthly reading gives 23 times and fails. The lower brackets alone already exceed BPS's total, so the thesis reports value shares as ranges.

Most online sellers do not use a marketplace. About 96 percent use chat or social media. Use of marketplaces fell every time it was measured, from 21.6 percent of sellers in 2020 to 17.2 percent in 2024. BPS worded the question slightly differently in the 2023 and 2024 rounds. From 2023 to 2024, BPS's marketplace value grew by 1.45 percent and other channels by 20.57 percent. So 98.5 percent of the increase came from outside the marketplaces. BPS's marketplace category includes many food and ride merchants: 43 percent of its sellers use only Gojek or Grab, and 47 percent use a shopping marketplace.

**Table 5.7. Marketplace use and financial statements (percent of sellers)**

| Year | Use a marketplace | Keep complete financial statements |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 21.6 | 23.5 |
| 2022 | 19.8 | 20.7 |
| 2023 | 17.8 | 15.2 |
| 2024 | 17.2 | |

Records are thinner outside the marketplaces. In 2023, 28.6 percent of marketplace sellers kept complete financial statements, against 12.3 percent of other sellers. Table 5.7 shows that the share of all sellers who keep them fell. The fall from 2020 to 2022 came while the survey appears to have counted the same kinds of seller. The fall in 2023 may partly reflect the chat-only sellers added to the count. Even among sellers who began before 2020, the share fell from 23.4 to 16.8 percent between the two surveys. Sellers without statements held between 26 and 40 percent of online value in 2022, depending on the scenario, against 79 percent of the count. The registered prediction was that their share of value would not fall by more than 5 points between 2020 and 2022. It fell by 5.8 points, so the prediction failed, narrowly. A second registered version of the test gives a fall of 3.3 points.

The World Bank surveys point the same way. In six Indonesian cities, 27 percent of unregistered firms sell through social media, and 70 percent keep no written records. Their median sales are about US$4,000 a year. The registered prediction on their mean sales failed: it was below about US$6,600, and the mean is about US$7,300. Among formal firms, 39.9 percent in Indonesia have neither a website nor online tax filing, the highest of seven countries. Malaysia has 9.1 percent and Thailand 8.7 percent.

## 5.11 Why the national figures disagree

The third objective asks whether platform, national and payment figures give the same account. They do not give the same account of scale. They give the same account of growth only where they cover the same sellers.

The comparison with Bank Indonesia is a registered test, and the other comparisons are descriptive. In 2023, Bank Indonesia's figure fell by 4.7 percent and BPS's rose by 40.6 percent. Where the two cover the same ground, they agree on slow growth. BPS's own marketplace slice grew by 0.2 percent from 2022 to 2023 in the middle scenario, and by 0.2 to 13.2 percent across the scenarios. It grew by 1.45 percent from 2023 to 2024. The registered test holds. The part of BPS's marketplace sales that went to final consumers in 2022 is 0.68 times Bank Indonesia's figure in the middle scenario. It is 0.16 times in the low scenario and 1.62 times in the high one. So the disagreement sits outside the marketplaces, in the count of sellers that only BPS reaches.

The 2024 figures show the same split. Those that cover mainly platforms grew by 5 to 7 percent, and BPS's grew by 17.1 percent. Adjacent figures point the same way as BPS, but they do not measure Indonesian e-commerce. Southeast Asian parcels grew by 25.2 percent, and the tax on foreign digital services by 24.9 percent.

Sizes cannot be reconciled. BPS's marketplace slice, about US$13 billion in 2024, is less than a quarter of the marketplace estimate of Momentum Works, about US$56.5 billion. Platform transaction value counts shipping, digital goods, travel and offline sales that a seller survey does not count in the same way. In 2022, sellers with revenue above about US$3.4 million a year were 0.5 percent of BPS's marketplace sellers. They held between 18 and 39 percent of the slice's value. Only 19 sample rows lie in that bracket. Counts also differ. Tokopedia alone said it had about 15 million merchants in 2022, while BPS counts about 0.6 million marketplace sellers.

Platform sales do not always keep pace with household spending. GoTo's on-demand transaction value grew more slowly than household spending in each of four quarters. They run from the third quarter of 2025 to the second quarter of 2026. In the last of them it grew by 2.0 percent against 8.3 percent, while its revenue grew by 20.5 percent. Grab and Shopee grew faster, but their figures cover the region. Payment figures such as QRIS are context only. They include in-person payments and the switch from cash.
