# Chapter 5. Results

This chapter follows the thesis from the platforms to the national figures. It first asks how much of the commerce a platform's revenue shows and whether revenue keeps pace with it. It then asks why the two parted in Indonesia and what that meant for 2023. The second half turns to the survey: where the growth in sellers came from, who the extra sellers were, and why the national figures disagree. Results marked registered were written down before the data were opened, and those marked exploratory were designed afterwards, as section 4.6 defines. Unmarked results are descriptive.

## 5.1 How much revenue leaves out

Take three major platforms in Indonesia in 2023: Tokopedia, Shopee and Grab. Together they carried US$43 billion of transaction value and kept US$3 billion as revenue. The difference, the invisible wedge, was US$40 billion. Revenue was about 7 percent of what passed through them, and their ecosystem ratio, the wedge divided by revenue and the author's own measure, was 12.7. For every dollar of revenue, almost 13 dollars of commerce passed through the platforms without counting as revenue. A thin slice fits how platforms are paid and how they book revenue. A platform that charges both sides can charge little on one of them (Rochet and Tirole 2003). Accounting rules also let an agent record only its own commission (International Accounting Standards Board 2014). Table 5.1 shows how each amount was obtained.

**Table 5.1. The invisible wedge of three platforms, 2023 (US$ billion)**

| Platform | Transaction value | Revenue | Wedge | How it is labelled |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tokopedia | 16.33 | 0.41 | 15.93 | Both direct segment disclosure |
| Shopee, Indonesia | 21.52 | 2.15 | 19.37 | Transaction value is an outside estimate (Momentum Works); revenue is derived at an assumed take rate of 10 percent |
| Grab, Indonesia | 5.38 | 0.60 | 4.78 | Revenue is a direct country disclosure; transaction value is derived from Grab's company-wide ratio |
| Total | 43.23 | 3.16 | 40.07 | Rounded amounts may not add |

Little of this depends on the assumptions. If Shopee kept 9 or 11 percent instead of 10, the total would stay between US$39.9 and US$40.3 billion. At Sea's company-wide rate of 11.5 percent it would be US$39.8 billion. Dropping any one platform leaves at least US$20.7 billion. Blibli's and Bukalapak's wedges, US$3.2 and US$10.5 billion, are left out because Blibli's measure includes online travel and Bukalapak reports for its whole group. For platforms that report both amounts directly, the wedge is 97 to 99 percent of transaction value in every year, so it moves almost one for one with it. Appendix A gives the wedge by platform and year.

Platforms are only part of online selling. In the BPS survey, the marketplace channel carries between 16 and 26 percent of online sales value, and BPS counts food and ride apps in that channel. So BPS places most online sales value outside the marketplace channel, and platform revenue shows less still. Section 5.9 sets the platform totals beside BPS's. A small slice can still be a fair guide if it keeps its size. The next question is whether it does.

## 5.2 Do revenue and transaction value grow together?

Among Indonesian and regional platforms they do not, and the thesis rejects H1. The typical take rate moved by about 20 percent in a year, up or down. At the typical foreign platform it moved by about 6 percent, so the difference is about threefold. Table 5.2 gives the main test, which is exploratory, and its checks.

**Table 5.2. The test of H1**

| Test | Method | Indonesian and regional group | Foreign group | p-value |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Yearly change in log take rate, 5 platforms against 27 | Mann–Whitney | 0.20 | 0.06 | 0.004 |
| The same | Shuffle | 0.20 | 0.06 | 0.022 |
| Growth divergence in points, 5 platforms against 26 | Mann–Whitney | 27.0 | 6.5 | 0.005 |
| Drop each platform in turn, or treat Tokopedia and GoTo as one company | Mann–Whitney | 0.17 to 0.23 | 0.06 | 0.003 to 0.016 |
| Quarterly pairs, 3 platforms against 8 | Mann–Whitney | 0.071 | 0.049 | 0.19 |

The first two rows are the main test, using the two methods of Chapter 4. The third row repeats it with growth divergence in points, and the fourth shows that no single platform carries the result. The planned test was narrower: the three Indonesian marketplaces against the eight foreign platforms chosen first. It rejects H1 when it compares pairs of years (p = 0.002), but pairs from one platform are not independent. When it compares platforms, which is the fairer count, it comes close without passing (p = 0.06), because three platforms give little power. The larger groups were set up after that first result, which is why the main test is exploratory. In the narrower test, revenue growth and transaction value growth had a correlation of 0.10 in Indonesia and 0.98 abroad, excluding Sea (Appendix B). The quarterly test in the last row does not reject H1. Its Indonesian pairs mostly cover 2023 to 2026, after most of the repricing, and the large moves came between 2021 and 2024. So the rejection describes that period.

The thin slice explains much of the threefold difference, which is an exploratory finding. A log change in the take rate is roughly the change in points divided by the take rate itself. Indonesian and regional platforms changed their take rates by about 1 point a year, as foreign platforms did. But the typical Indonesian and regional platform keeps only 1.6 percent of each sale, against 16.9 percent abroad. Among the foreign platforms the share runs from 2.6 percent at Shopify to 71 percent at Zalando, which also sells goods itself. If transaction value is flat, a rise of 1 point lifts revenue by more than half at a platform that keeps 1.6 percent. It lifts revenue by about 6 percent at one that keeps 16.9 percent. The larger the wedge is next to revenue, the more a small price change moves revenue growth. Figure 5.1 shows the three measures side by side. What the platforms actually changed is the next question.

![Figure 5.1](figures/fig5_1_thin_slice.png)

## 5.3 What moved the take rate

Tokopedia shows it best. In 2023 its transaction value fell by 9 percent while its revenue rose by 53 percent. Across the three Indonesian marketplaces, the take rate accounts for two thirds of the movement in revenue at the median (66.5 percent). At the eight foreign platforms chosen first, it accounts for about a quarter (26.7 percent). The wedge follows the volume instead: at the median, about 96 percent of a change in the wedge comes from the change in transaction value, in both groups.

Table 5.3 splits the growth of revenue into more commerce, higher fees and lower incentives, which are the discounts and vouchers a platform pays for. Fees and incentives are measured per unit of transaction value. A negative share means that the part pulled revenue down. The windows of one platform overlap, so they are not seven independent cases.

**Table 5.3. What moved revenue in seven Indonesian and regional windows (share of the growth of revenue, percent)**

| Platform and window | Growth of revenue | More commerce | Higher fees | Lower incentives |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tokopedia, 2022–23 | +53% | −22 | 76 | 45 |
| Blibli third-party retail, 2022–23 | +465% | 17 | 20 | 63 |
| Blibli third-party retail, 2022–25 | +679% | 18 | 63 | 20 |
| Grab on-demand, 2021–24 | +321% | 34 | 34 | 33 |
| Grab on-demand, 2022–24 | +86% | 42 | 11 | 47 |
| GoTo on-demand, 2022–25 | +111% | 12 | 36 | 52 |
| GoTo on-demand, 2023–25 | +114% | 27 | 6 | 67 |

Lower incentives were the larger of the two take-rate parts in four windows and higher fees in two. In the seventh, Grab from 2021 to 2024, the two were nearly equal. Shares are rounded, so a row can add to 99 or 101. Two series need a note. GoTo's 2022 transaction value is an estimate. Blibli does not report incentives, so its split rests on a proxy, and part of its 2022–23 rise comes from travel and digital products. Bukalapak's reports do not allow the parts to be separated.

Tokopedia needs two views. Accounting rules normally deduct what a company pays its customers from its revenue (International Accounting Standards Board 2014), and Tokopedia's incentives fell by about one third in 2023. Counting the lines of its accounts, 61 percent of the extra net revenue came from lower incentives and 39 percent from higher gross fees. That count leaves the fall in volume inside both lines. Table 5.3 takes it out, and on that per-unit basis higher fees account for more of the rise in the take rate than lower incentives. Both views agree that pricing and not more commerce drove the rise, and they differ only on which lever led (Figure 5.2). Across these windows, revenue growth was not a fair guide to the commerce the platforms carried. Did investors see through it?

**Figure 5.2. Tokopedia, 2022 to 2023: two ways to split the rise in revenue.** Left: the change in net revenue split by the lines of the accounts. Right: the growth of revenue split per unit of transaction value, where a negative share pulled revenue down. Source: GoTo annual report 2023, segment note; author's calculations.

## 5.4 What markets valued

Market value moved with transaction value, a registered result. Across 28 listed platforms and 138 platform-years, a 1 percent rise in transaction value goes with a 0.6 percent rise in market value (p = 0.002). The second prediction was that markets value growth from a higher take rate less. It did not hold in the main sample. A 1 percent rise in the take rate goes with a rise of 0.3 percent. That estimate is too uncertain to tell from zero. The difference between the two is not large enough to count (p = 0.20). Trimming extreme values leaves the difference at p = 0.16. A second run adds six series, three segments and three more whole companies, and gives 0.63 for transaction value and 0.07 for the take rate. There the difference counts (p = 0.004). That run mixes segment figures with whole-company market values, as the registration warned, so the thesis does not lean on it. The answer depends on which platforms are in the sample.

The sample differs from the 27 foreign platforms of H1. It adds Airbnb, Booking, DoorDash, Lyft and Uber, and it lacks Despegar, Mercado Libre, Ozon, Rakuten and Sea, which enter only the second run or not at all. So the data show that market value follows the commerce a platform carries. Early studies of internet companies found that markets price web-traffic measures too, which points the same way (Trueman, Wong and Zhang 2000; Rajgopal, Venkatachalam and Kotha 2003). The data do not show that markets saw through the pricing effect. Both results are associations and not stock returns, because market value also changes when a company issues shares. The next section asks what the commerce did in 2023.

## 5.5 The platforms and the sellers in 2023

Buying through platforms did not boom in 2023. Figure 5.3 sets six measures of it against household spending, which rose by 9.4 percent in current prices. Five of the six grew more slowly, and three fell. Blibli is the exception.

![Figure 5.3](figures/fig5_3_growth_2023.png)

**Figure 5.3. Growth in 2023 (percent).** Each measure is in its own unit. Sources: company reports, Bank Indonesia, Momentum Works and BPS; household spending from national accounts. GoTo's change rests on an estimated 2022 value, and Momentum Works' 3.5 is the author's calculation from its published levels (its 2024 release states 3.7). Appendix D gives the values.

Because the measures are in different units, the thesis compares only their rates. The blue bars show the buying, and the orange bars show the figures that described a boom. Revenue and BPS's value rose strongly while the buying through platforms did not.

Sellers did not describe a boom either. BPS asks each business how its online sales changed. Among businesses already selling, the median change was zero. A registered prediction had set the limit at 10.4 percent, the published growth in sales per business. A share of 24 percent reported a rise, 32 percent a fall and 44 percent no change, against 37 percent up and 24 percent down a year earlier. The mean change was −4.3 percent, and lack of demand became the main obstacle, named by 41 percent of sellers against 35 percent in 2022. Yet the published count of online sellers rose by 27 percent that year. Where did those sellers come from?

## 5.6 Where the extra sellers came from

On BPS's published figures, H2 holds. From 2022 to 2023, online sales value grew by 40.6 percent, the number of businesses by 27.4 percent and sales per business by 10.4 percent. So more businesses account for 71 percent of the growth. From 2023 to 2024 the figures were 17.1, 15.3 and 1.5 percent, and more businesses account for 90 percent, or 71 percent on BPS's other count for 2023. The split is arithmetic, and it cannot tell a new seller from one counted for the first time.

The entry test can, and its result is registered. In the 2024 file, 13.45 percent of sellers began selling online in 2023, and the rule required at least 21.5 percent. The test fails. Even if no seller stopped, that many new sellers lets the count grow by at most 15.5 percent over the sellers already there, against the published 27.4 percent. Respondents often place past events nearer to the interview than they were (Neter and Waksberg 1964). If sellers who began earlier reported 2023, that would make new sellers look more numerous, so the shortfall is if anything understated.

**Table 5.4. The 2023 rise in the count of online sellers (thousands)**

| Part of the rise | If no seller stopped | If sellers stopped at 5 percent a year |
|---|---|---|
| Rise between the weighted totals of the two files | 819 (100%) | 819 (100%) |
| Net entry: sellers who began in 2023, less those who stopped | 513 (63%) | 361 (44%) |
| Earlier sellers newly counted above the whole 2022 count | 306 (37%) | 458 (56%) |

Source: BPS survey files, weighted; author's calculations.

Table 5.4 shows the accounting. The rise of 819 thousand is the gap between the weighted totals of the two files, and BPS's own published counts give 821 thousand (BPS 2025). The 2023 survey counted 306 thousand more sellers who began before 2023 than the whole 2022 count held. That is 37 percent of the rise if no seller stopped and 56 percent if sellers stopped at 5 percent a year. So about half of the rise is not explained by first-time entry. This accounting and the checks below are exploratory, and they point the same way.

Cohorts behave as they should, except in 2023. Sellers who began by 2020 numbered 9.9 percent fewer in the 2022 survey than in the 2020 one, over two years. In the 2023 survey they numbered 6.0 percent more than in the 2022 one. Between the 2023 and 2024 surveys, the cohort that began before 2020 shrank by 10.2 percent, and about 750 thousand new sellers appeared against a rise of 584 thousand. The break is also uneven. Across the 34 provinces, count growth had almost no link to the share of new sellers (0.10) and a weak link to growth in sample size (0.35). These are rank correlations, which compare the order of provinces on two measures and run from −1 to 1. Sellers who began by 2022 and use only chat or social media rose by 13 percent, while marketplace users rose by 0.3 percent. BPS's published error rates put the standard error of the national count, the usual size of its sampling noise, at about 1.4 percent. The 306 thousand sellers are about 4.6 times the standard error of the change. That calculation applies the 2024 edition's error rates to both years and treats the two surveys as independent.

The value side tells a similar story. BPS's value rose from about US$53 billion to about US$72 billion. Sellers not explained by entry account for 15 to 17 percent of this, and entrants for 25 to 30 percent. The remaining 53 to 59 percent, about US$11 to 12 billion at the 2023 exchange rate, cannot be traced to existing sellers' answers, which show a mean change of −4.3 percent. These checks point to wider survey coverage of chat and social sellers. That is the pattern survey research warns about, because a change in how a survey finds respondents can break a series (van den Brakel, Smith and Compton 2008). The data cannot separate it from sellers returning after a pause. Who were the extra sellers?

## 5.7 Who the extra sellers were, and whether they returned

The comparison of who they were is exploratory. The surveys do not follow the same businesses, so the thesis compares groups. For each group it takes the 2023 count above what its normal yearly change in 2020 to 2022 would give. These extra sellers total about 450 thousand, close to the 458 thousand in Table 5.4. Figure 5.4 gives each group's share of this extra count next to its share of the 2022 count.

![Figure 5.4](figures/fig5_4_extra_sellers.png)

**Figure 5.4. Who the extra sellers were.** The extra sellers are the 2023 count above what each group's normal yearly change in 2020 to 2022 would give, about 450 thousand in total. Source: BPS survey files, weighted; author's calculations. Exploratory.

The extra count leaned toward long-running businesses, older owners and places outside Java, and the lean toward men in the raw data disappears once normal change is taken out. It does not look like the young start-ups of the usual growth story. That raises one question: could these be established sellers who paused and came back?

A returning seller would usually sell for only part of the year, unless the seller restarts in January, and part-year selling fell in 2023. Among sellers who began by 2021, the number selling for part of the year fell from 472 thousand to 318 thousand. It fell for the groups who began by 2020 and by 2022 as well. Small firms that stop are more often young, with young owners (McKenzie and Paffhausen 2019). If returners were mostly such firms, the extra count would lean young. It leaned old. Returners equal to 10 to 15 percent of the 2022 count would remove the unexplained part, which is a scenario and not an estimate. The check cannot rule out returners. The files carry no linked histories, BPS changed how it asks about months between the rounds, and exits or a move to full-year selling could offset them.

## 5.8 Channels and records

Two additional analyses show where these sellers sit. The path of marketplace use, the value held by sellers without statements and the World Bank results were registered. The comparison within the pre-2020 group is exploratory.

Most online sellers do not use a marketplace, and about 96 percent use chat or social media. Use of marketplaces fell every time it was measured, from 21.6 percent of sellers in 2020 to 17.2 percent in 2024. BPS worded the question slightly differently for 2023 and 2024 than for the earlier years. From 2023 to 2024, BPS's marketplace value grew by 1.45 percent and other channels by 20.57 percent, so 98.5 percent of the increase came from outside the marketplaces. BPS's marketplace category includes many food and ride merchants: 43 percent of its sellers use only Gojek or Grab, and 47 percent use a shopping marketplace.

![Figure 5.5](figures/fig5_5_marketplace_and_records.png)

**Figure 5.5. Marketplace use and financial statements, 2020 to 2024.** Marketplace use for 2020, 2022 and 2023 comes from the survey files and for 2024 from BPS's published figure. Financial statements come from the survey files. BPS worded the marketplace question slightly differently for 2023 and 2024, and the survey has no point for 2021. Source: BPS; author's calculations.

Records are thinner outside the marketplaces. In 2023, 28.6 percent of marketplace sellers kept complete financial statements, against 12.3 percent of other sellers, and Figure 5.5 shows the share of all sellers who keep them falling. The fall from 2020 to 2022 came while the survey appears to have counted the same kinds of seller. The fall in 2023 may partly reflect the chat-only sellers added to the count. Even among sellers who began before 2020, the share fell from 23.4 percent in 2022 to 16.8 percent in 2023.

Value shares rest on the bracket scenarios of section 4.4. The annual reading passes the validation rule, because the middle scenario gives 1.95 times BPS's total for 2022, and the monthly reading fails at 23 times. The lower brackets alone already exceed BPS's total, so the shares are ranges. Sellers without statements held between 26 and 40 percent of online value in 2022, against 79 percent of the count. The registered prediction was that their share of value would not fall by more than 5 points between 2020 and 2022. It fell by 5.8 points, so the prediction failed, narrowly. A second registered version gives a fall of 3.3 points, which is inside the limit.

The World Bank surveys point the same way. In six Indonesian cities, 27 percent of unregistered firms use social media with their customers. Among those, 70 percent keep no written records, and their median sales are about US$4,000 a year. The registered prediction on their mean sales failed: it was below about US$6,600, and the mean is about US$7,300. Among formal firms, 39.9 percent in Indonesia have neither a website nor online tax filing, the highest of seven countries, against 9.1 percent in Malaysia and 8.7 percent in Thailand.

## 5.9 Why the national figures disagree

Chapter 1 began with two official figures that moved in opposite directions in 2023. The third objective asks whether platform, national and payment figures give the same account. They do not give the same account of scale. They give the same account of growth only where they cover the same sellers.

In 2023, Bank Indonesia's figure fell by 4.7 percent and BPS's rose by 40.6 percent. Where the two cover the same ground, the growth is small. BPS's own marketplace slice grew by 0.2 percent from 2022 to 2023 in the middle scenario, and by 0.2 to 13.2 percent across the scenarios. It grew by 1.45 percent from 2023 to 2024. A registered test gives a mixed answer on size. The part of BPS's marketplace sales that went to final consumers in 2022 is 0.68 times Bank Indonesia's figure in the middle scenario and 0.16 times in the low one. In the high scenario it is 1.62 times. The prediction was that it would fall below Bank Indonesia's figure, so it holds in the first two scenarios and fails in the third. Most of the disagreement then sits outside the marketplaces, in the count of sellers that only BPS reaches.

The 2024 figures show the same split. Those that cover mainly platforms grew by 5 to 7 percent, and BPS's grew by 17.1 percent. Adjacent figures point the same way as BPS but do not measure Indonesian e-commerce. Southeast Asian parcels grew by 25.2 percent, and the tax on foreign digital services by 24.9 percent.

Sizes cannot be reconciled. In 2023, BPS's marketplace slice was about US$13 billion, against US$38 billion for Tokopedia and the Shopee estimate together. BPS's whole total for 2023, US$72 billion, lies above the three platforms' US$43 billion in Table 5.1, but the two count different things. In 2024 it was less than a quarter of Momentum Works' marketplace estimate of about US$56.5 billion. Platform transaction value counts shipping, digital goods, travel and offline sales that a seller survey does not count in the same way. Counts differ too: Tokopedia alone said in a company statement that it had about 15 million merchants in 2022, while BPS counts about 0.6 million marketplace sellers.

Platform sales do not always keep pace with household spending either. GoTo's on-demand transaction value grew more slowly than household spending in each of four quarters, from the third quarter of 2025 to the second quarter of 2026. In the last it grew by 2.0 percent against 8.3 percent, while its revenue grew by 20.5 percent. Grab and Shopee grew faster, but their figures cover the region. Payment figures such as QRIS are context only, because they include in-person payments and the switch from cash.

Taken together, the results answer the research question at two levels. Revenue showed a small part of the commerce the platforms carried, and in the windows traced it was not a fair guide to that commerce, because the take rate moved. The official count of online sellers followed first-time entry only in part in 2023, and the platforms and the survey disagree most where only the survey looks. Chapter 6 asks what this means for the rules that Indonesia is writing.
