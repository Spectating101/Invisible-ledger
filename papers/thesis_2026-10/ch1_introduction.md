# Chapter 1. Introduction

Digital commerce has changed how goods and services are bought and sold in Indonesia. Sellers in small towns reach buyers across the country, businesses run from mobile phones, and customers pay by QR code. The figures that measure this activity disagree on both its size and its growth.

In 2023, traders at Tanah Abang, a major wholesale market in Jakarta, said their customers had moved to sellers on TikTok Shop (AFP 2023). The government then banned transactions on social media platforms, and TikTok Shop closed in Indonesia soon after. That year the national statistics office, BPS, reported that online sales rose by about 41 percent (BPS 2025). The central bank's measure of online shopping fell by about 5 percent (Bank Indonesia 2025). The two official figures moved in opposite directions.

Tokopedia, an Indonesian online marketplace, shows the same split inside one company. Between 2022 and 2023, its revenue rose by 53 percent while the value of transactions on the platform, its transaction value, fell by 9 percent (GoTo Gojek Tokopedia 2024). The two are different amounts. A platform keeps only part of each sale, as commissions and fees. The rest is the seller's receipts, the driver's fare or another amount that passes through. This thesis calls the difference the invisible wedge. The term means what a revenue-only measure leaves out. It does not mean activity that goes unrecorded.

The share a platform keeps is not fixed. When it rises, revenue can grow even while transaction value falls. In Tokopedia's accounts, about 60 percent of the extra revenue came from smaller discounts and incentives, which a platform pays for out of what it earns.

Money and rules rest on these figures, and not only in Indonesia. Businesses in 43 economies sold almost US$27 trillion online in 2022, by the United Nations' estimate (UNCTAD 2024), and platforms carry a growing part of that trade. Investors value the companies that run them largely on reported growth. If part of that growth is a platform keeping more of each sale, then part of the price is a pricing decision. Governments use the same figures to size their digital economies and to tax online sellers.

Research explains why the figures can differ, but not when a figure stops moving with the activity it represents, or by how much. Answering that takes two things that are rarely found together: a platform's revenue and transaction value over several years, and a national count of online sellers whose underlying answers can be examined.

Indonesia offers both. Several of its platforms report revenue and transaction value each year, and BPS asks online sellers when they began and how their sales changed. The stakes are concrete. For 2024, BPS and the central bank give about US$81 billion and about US$31 billion for online sales (BPS 2025; Bank Indonesia 2025), a difference of more than two to one. The government is writing rules on platform commissions, discounts and tax. Its levy on marketplace sellers would raise about US$60 million a year on one measure of the market and about US$300 million on another, before exemptions (Ministry of Finance of the Republic of Indonesia 2025; author's calculation). Most online sellers work through messaging and social media, outside the marketplaces, so platform and survey figures are likely to part. The events of 2023 suggest that they did.

## 1.1 Research question and objectives

This thesis sets out to change how the digital economy is measured. Most online commerce is missing from the platform figures on which companies are valued and sellers are taxed, and millions of the people who sell online are outside those figures altogether. Decisions worth trillions of dollars rest on numbers that show a fraction of the activity they are taken to describe. The ambition of this thesis is to bring the rest into view: to show how much of the online economy the current figures miss, where it is, and who is in it.

Measuring what the current figures miss begins with one question: how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth? The question has two halves. The first concerns size, which is how much of the activity lies outside the figure. The second concerns growth, which is whether the figure still moves with the activity once prices or coverage change. A large wedge would matter less if it stayed in proportion; the second half asks whether it did.

The research question is addressed through three objectives. The first is to measure the invisible wedge across the platform histories available from 2020 to 2025. The second is to reconcile selected large movements with disclosed changes in monetization, customer incentives, revenue recognition and reporting scope. The third is to compare the platform evidence with national e-commerce and payment figures, to test whether they give the same account of economic scale and growth.

The thesis tests two hypotheses. H1 says that a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value. H2 says that national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online, and less from higher sales per business. An entry test then asks whether a rise in the count of online sellers comes from sellers entering for the first time. A further analysis estimates how many sellers a rule built on platform records can reach.

In brief, revenue shows only a small part of the commerce that platforms carry, and in BPS's survey the marketplaces carry only about a fifth of online sales value. In Indonesia, platform revenue also grew faster than the commerce behind it, mainly because platforms kept a larger share of each sale and not because buyers bought more. National growth came mainly from more sellers, as H2 states. But about half of the 2023 rise in the count of sellers is not explained by first-time entry.

## 1.2 Contributions

The thesis makes three contributions, one for each group that relies on these figures: investors, statistics offices, and the authorities that tax and regulate online selling.

For those who value platforms, the thesis shows how small a part of the commerce a platform's revenue records. It shows why this matters for growth as well as scale: the larger the wedge is next to revenue, the more a small change in fees or discounts moves revenue growth. It also traces why revenue moved, which gives investors a check on whether reported growth came from more commerce or from a larger share of each sale.

For those who measure the economy, the thesis shows where the commerce outside the platforms sits and tests what a rise in the count of online sellers represents. Most online sellers are small businesses that keep no financial statements. Each reports the year it began selling online, so the survey shows how much of a rise is first-time entry. The test was specified before the survey files were opened.

For those who design tax and regulation, the thesis measures how far rules based on platform records reach. A rule built on platform records reaches only the sellers who use platforms, so where most sellers work outside them, such rules cover a minority. The thesis also sets each recent rule, from the cap on ride-hailing commissions to the limit on discounts, against the figure it acts on. Several other countries have adopted similar rules, so the same question applies there.

Earlier work has already compared platform transaction values with national e-commerce statistics. The contribution of this thesis is the connected evidence for one country, drawn from the platforms' accounts, the sellers' own survey responses and the rules that act on those figures.

## 1.3 Structure of the thesis

The thesis moves from ideas to evidence and then to what follows from it. Chapter 2 reviews why platform revenue differs from the commerce behind it, how online selling is measured, and what platform records can show. It ends with the questions the literature leaves open. Chapter 3 turns those questions into measures, two hypotheses and the entry test. Chapter 4 describes the data, which come from platform reports, BPS survey files and national and market figures, and the tests that use them. Chapter 5 gives the results, first for the platforms and then for the national figures. Chapter 6 asks how far Indonesia's recent rules reach and what the findings mean for other countries. Chapter 7 concludes and states the limits of the evidence.
