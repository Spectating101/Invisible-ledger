# Chapter 1. Introduction

Digital commerce has changed how goods and services are bought and sold in Indonesia. Sellers in small towns reach buyers across the country, businesses operate from mobile phones, and payments are made by QR code. The figures used to measure this activity, however, differ on both its size and its growth.

The difference was visible in 2023. Traders at Tanah Abang, a major wholesale market in Jakarta, reported that their customers had moved to sellers on TikTok Shop. The government then banned transactions on social media platforms, and TikTok Shop closed in Indonesia shortly afterwards. The national statistics office, BPS, later reported that online sales rose by about 41 percent that year. The central bank's measure of online shopping fell by about 5 percent over the same period.

The same divergence appears within a single company. Between 2022 and 2023, Tokopedia's revenue rose by 53 percent while the value of transactions on the platform fell by 9 percent. Both figures come from the company's own reports. Measured by revenue, the platform grew; measured by transaction value, it contracted.

These figures differ because they measure different things. A platform that handles 100 units of sales may keep only a small part as its own revenue, in the form of commissions and fees. The rest belongs to the sale itself: the seller's receipts, the driver's fare and other amounts that pass through. An analyst reading the platform's accounts sees the small part, while one measuring the commerce it carries sees the whole. This thesis calls the difference the invisible wedge. The term refers to what a revenue-only measure leaves out, not to activity that goes unrecorded.

The choice of figure matters because different readers rely on different ones. Investors value platforms largely on their revenue. Statistics offices measure online selling through surveys of businesses. Tax authorities increasingly work from the records that platforms keep about their sellers, and several governments now collect tax from online sellers through the platforms. When the figures move apart, these readers can reach opposite conclusions about the same year.

Earlier research explains why revenue and transaction value differ. Platforms set prices on both sides of a market, and accounting rules decide how much of each sale counts as revenue. There is less evidence on how large the difference becomes in practice, whether it stays stable, and how far platform figures represent a country's online selling as a whole.

Indonesia allows these questions to be examined directly. It is the largest digital market in Southeast Asia, and several of its platforms report both their transaction value and their revenue. Most of its online sellers also work outside the marketplaces, through messaging and social media applications. BPS surveys these sellers regularly and asks when they began selling online and how their sales changed. This thesis uses the platforms' reports, a comparison group of 27 listed platforms in other countries, and the BPS survey responses for three years.

## 1.1 Research question and objectives

One question drives the thesis: how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth?

Three objectives follow, unchanged from the proposal. The first is to measure the invisible wedge across the platform histories available from 2020 to 2025. The second is to reconcile selected large movements with disclosed changes in monetization, customer incentives, revenue recognition and reporting scope. The third is to compare the platform evidence with national e-commerce and payment indicators, to test whether they give the same account of economic scale and growth.

Two hypotheses test the links between these figures. H1 states that a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value. H2 states that national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online, and less from higher sales per business. Chapter 3 sets out both in full.

## 1.2 Main findings

Revenue shows only a small part of the commerce that platforms carry. Of every 100 rupiah spent through Indonesia's large platforms, about 7 appear as revenue, and in 2023 the invisible wedge on three platforms was about US$40 billion. The platforms themselves carry about a fifth of online sales, so a revenue-based measure covers a small share of a minority of online selling.

Revenue also did not grow in proportion to transaction value, and H1 is rejected. The share that Indonesian platforms keep moved about three times as much, relative to its size, as the share kept by platforms abroad. The price changes behind this were of similar size in both groups. Indonesian marketplaces keep a much smaller share of each sale, so a change of the same size moved their revenue far more. Much of the change came from platforms paying for fewer discounts, as Tokopedia's accounts show.

The year 2023 illustrates the consequence. Revenue rose while most measures of buying through the platforms grew more slowly than household spending, and some fell. The sellers surveyed by BPS mostly reported flat or lower online sales, and their most common difficulty was a lack of customers.

The national seller count requires the same care. On BPS's published figures, growth came mainly from more businesses, and H2 holds. The survey responses change how that result should be read. About half of the increase in sellers in 2023 is not explained by businesses selling online for the first time. The additional count was concentrated among older, long-established small businesses that sell through messaging and social media.

These findings bear on current policy. Indonesia's new marketplace tax relies on platform records, and it can collect from about one online seller in twenty. Most online sellers work outside the marketplaces and fall outside its reach.

## 1.3 Contributions

The thesis makes three contributions. First, it shows why the invisible wedge matters for growth and not only for size. The larger the wedge is relative to revenue, the more a given price change moves revenue growth. This explains why revenue is a weaker guide to transaction activity where platforms keep a small share of each sale.

Second, it tests what an increase in a national count of online sellers represents. Because each seller reports the year they began selling online, the survey shows how much of an increase is first-time entry. The test was specified before the survey files were opened, and it is applied here to Indonesia's 2023 count.

Third, it measures how far rules based on platform records reach. Where most sellers work outside the platforms, such rules cover a minority of them. This is relevant beyond Indonesia, because several countries have adopted similar rules.

Earlier work has already compared platform transaction values with national e-commerce statistics. The contribution of this thesis is the connected evidence for one country, drawn from the platforms' accounts, the sellers' survey responses and the rules built on both.

## 1.4 Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2 reviews the literature on platform revenue, the measurement of online selling and the use of platform records for tax. Chapter 3 defines the measures and develops the hypotheses. Chapter 4 describes the data and the tests. Chapter 5 presents the results, beginning with the platforms and moving to the national figures.
