# Chapter 1. Introduction

Digital commerce has changed how goods and services are bought and sold in Indonesia. Sellers in small towns reach buyers across the country, businesses operate from mobile phones, and payments are made by QR code. The figures used to measure this activity, however, differ on both its size and its growth.

In 2023, traders at Tanah Abang, a major wholesale market in Jakarta, reported that their customers had moved to sellers on TikTok Shop. The government then banned transactions on social media platforms, and TikTok Shop closed in Indonesia shortly afterwards. The national statistics office, BPS, later reported that online sales rose by about 41 percent that year. The central bank's measure of online shopping fell by about 5 percent over the same period. The two official figures moved in opposite directions.

Tokopedia, an Indonesian online marketplace, shows the same split inside a single company. Between 2022 and 2023, its revenue rose by 53 percent while the value of transactions on the platform fell by 9 percent. Both figures come from the company's own reports. Measured by revenue, the platform grew; measured by transaction value, it contracted.

Revenue and transaction value differ because a platform keeps only part of each sale, and that part can change. A platform that carries 100 units of transaction value may keep only a small part as its own revenue, in the form of commissions and fees. The rest belongs to the sale itself: the seller's receipts on a marketplace, the driver's fare on a ride-hailing app, and other amounts that pass through. An analyst reading the platform's accounts sees the small part, while one measuring the commerce it carries sees the whole. This thesis calls the difference the invisible wedge. The term refers to what a revenue-only measure leaves out, not to activity that goes unrecorded.

The share of each sale that a platform keeps is not fixed. When it rises, revenue can grow even while transaction value falls. At Tokopedia, about 60 percent of the rise in revenue reflected smaller discounts and incentives, which a platform pays for out of what it earns.

Money and public decisions rest on figures like these, and not only in Indonesia. Businesses in 43 economies sold almost US$27 trillion online in 2022, by the United Nations' estimate, and platforms carry a growing part of that trade. The companies that run them are among the most valuable in the world, and they are valued largely on the growth they report. If part of that growth comes from a platform keeping more of each sale, and not from more commerce, then part of what investors pay for is a pricing decision. Governments face the same uncertainty. They use these figures to judge how large their digital economies are, and a growing number now ask platforms to report what their sellers earn, or to collect tax from them. A rule built on platform records, for instance, reaches only the sellers who use platforms. Each of these readers is acting on a number without knowing how far it can be relied on.

Existing research gives investors and governments limited help in checking the figures they use. It explains why the figures differ: platforms set prices on both sides of a market, accounting rules decide how much of each sale counts as revenue, and national accounts separate a platform's own service from the commerce it carries. Statistical agencies have also begun to set business surveys beside platform transaction values. What that research does not show is when a figure stops moving with the activity it is used to represent, or by how much. Answering that requires two things that are rarely available together: a platform's revenue and transaction value reported over several years, and a national count of online sellers whose underlying answers can be examined.

Indonesia is one of the few markets where both kinds of evidence are available. Several of its platforms report their transaction value and their revenue each year, and BPS asks online sellers when they began selling and how their sales changed. The stakes are also concrete there. For 2024, BPS and the central bank give the two main official estimates of online sales, about US$81 billion and about US$31 billion. The size of the market is uncertain by a factor of more than two. The government is nevertheless writing rules for that market, on platform commissions, on discounts and on tax. Its new levy on marketplace sellers would raise about US$60 million a year on one measure of the market and about US$300 million on another, before exemptions. Indonesia is also a market where platform figures and survey figures are likely to part, because most of its online sellers work outside the marketplaces, through messaging and social media applications. The events of 2023 suggest that they did part.

## 1.1 Research question and objectives

This thesis sets out to change how the digital economy is measured. Most online commerce is missing from the platform figures on which companies are valued and sellers are taxed, and millions of the people who sell online are outside those figures altogether. Decisions worth trillions of dollars rest on numbers that show a fraction of the activity they are taken to describe. The ambition of this thesis is to bring the rest into view: to show how much of the online economy the current figures miss, where it is, and who is in it.

Measuring what the current figures miss begins with one question: how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth? The question has two halves. The first concerns size, which is how much of the activity lies outside the figure. The second concerns growth, which is whether the figure still moves with the activity once prices or coverage change. A large wedge would matter less if it stayed in proportion; the second half asks whether it did.

The research question is addressed through three objectives. The first is to measure the invisible wedge across the platform histories available from 2020 to 2025. The second is to reconcile selected large movements with disclosed changes in monetization, customer incentives, revenue recognition and reporting scope. The third is to compare the platform evidence with national e-commerce and payment figures, to test whether they give the same account of economic scale and growth.

Two hypotheses and an entry test ask whether revenue and the national figures move with the activity behind them. H1 states that a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value. H2 states that national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online, and less from higher sales per business. The entry test asks whether a rise in the count of online sellers comes from sellers entering for the first time. A further analysis estimates how many sellers a rule based on platform records can reach.

In brief, the thesis finds that revenue shows only a small part of the commerce platforms carry, and that platforms carry only about a fifth of online sales. Both revenue and the official count of online sellers also overstated growth in the period studied. Platform revenue rose mainly because platforms kept a larger share of each sale, not because buyers bought more. National growth came mainly from more sellers, as H2 states, but about half of the 2023 rise in the count is not explained by first-time entry.

## 1.2 Contributions

The thesis makes three contributions, one for each group that relies on these figures: investors, statistics offices, and the authorities that tax and regulate online selling. Together they show how much the figures miss, where it sits, and who is in it.

For those who value platforms, the thesis shows how small a part of the commerce a platform's revenue records, and why that matters for growth and not only for scale. The larger the wedge is relative to revenue, the more a given change in fees or discounts moves revenue growth. It also traces why revenue moved, mostly to smaller discounts and higher fees. This gives investors a direct check on reported growth: whether it came from more commerce or from the platform keeping a larger share of each sale.

For those who measure the economy, the thesis shows where the commerce outside the platforms sits and tests what a rise in the count of online sellers represents. Most online sellers work through messaging and social media, and most are small businesses that keep no financial statements. Each seller in the survey reports the year they began selling online, so the survey itself shows how much of a rise is first-time entry. The test was specified before the survey files were opened.

For those who design tax and regulation, the thesis measures how far rules based on platform records reach. It also sets each recent rule, from the cap on ride-hailing commissions to the limit on discounts, against the figure it acts on. Where most sellers work outside the platforms, such rules cover a minority of them, and the revenue they can raise depends on which measure of the market is correct. Several other countries have adopted similar rules, so the same question applies there.

Earlier work has already compared platform transaction values with national e-commerce statistics. The contribution of this thesis is the connected evidence for one country, drawn from the platforms' accounts, the sellers' own survey responses and the rules that act on those figures.

## 1.3 Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2 reviews the literature on platform revenue, the measurement of online selling and the use of platform records for tax. Chapter 3 defines the measures, develops the hypotheses and sets out the entry test. Chapter 4 describes the data and the tests, including how revenue movements are traced and how the platform evidence is compared with national figures. Chapter 5 presents the results, beginning with the platforms and moving to the national figures. Chapter 6 examines how far Indonesia's recent rules reach and what the findings imply for other countries. Chapter 7 concludes and states the limitations.
