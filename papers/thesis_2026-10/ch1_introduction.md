# Chapter 1. Introduction

Digital commerce has changed how goods and services are bought and sold. A growing part of it passes through platforms, such as online marketplaces and ride and delivery apps. Investors value platforms largely on their reported growth, and governments use the same figures to size their digital economies and to tax online sellers. The trade is large: businesses in 43 economies sold almost US$27 trillion online in 2022, by the United Nations' estimate (UNCTAD 2024).

Investors and governments each treat a figure as a stand-in for the commerce behind it, and a stand-in follows the commerce only while the link between them holds. A platform keeps just part of each sale, as commissions and fees, and the rest is the seller's receipts, the driver's fare or another amount that passes through. The share a platform keeps is not fixed, so revenue can grow even when the commerce it carries falls. National figures face a similar problem, because a survey can reach more, or different, businesses from one year to the next.

In 2023, Indonesia's figures moved away from the commerce they stand for. Tokopedia, an Indonesian online marketplace, saw its net revenue rise by 53 percent. Its transaction value, the value of what was bought through the platform, fell by 9 percent (GoTo Gojek Tokopedia 2024a, 2024b). Nationally, the statistics office, BPS, reported that online sales rose by about 41 percent (BPS 2025a), while the central bank's measure of online shopping fell by about 5 percent (Bank Indonesia 2023; KabarBursa 2024).

At Tokopedia, about 60 percent of the extra revenue came from smaller discounts and incentives, which a platform pays out of its own earnings (GoTo Gojek Tokopedia 2024b). So the share the platform kept had changed, not only the commerce. This thesis calls what a revenue-only measure leaves out the invisible wedge. It does not mean activity that goes unrecorded.

Research explains why platform revenue and national sales figures can differ. It does not say when a figure stops moving with the activity it represents, or by how much. Answering that takes a platform's revenue and transaction value over several years, and a national count of online sellers whose underlying answers can be examined.

Indonesia allows the question to be tested. Several platforms report revenue and transaction value each year, and BPS asks online sellers when they began and how their sales changed. The two sources already differ on scale: for 2024, BPS gives about US$81 billion for online sales and the central bank about US$31 billion (BPS 2025b; Bank Indonesia 2025). New rules on platform commissions, discounts and tax act on these figures.

Most online sellers work through messaging and social media, outside the marketplaces, so platform and survey figures are likely to disagree. In 2023, traders at Tanah Abang, a wholesale market in Jakarta, said their customers had moved to sellers on TikTok Shop (Agence France-Presse 2023). The government then banned transactions on social media platforms, and TikTok Shop closed in Indonesia soon after (Ministry of Trade of the Republic of Indonesia 2023; Mandagie 2023). The events of 2023 suggest that they did.

## 1.1 Research question and objectives

This thesis sets out to change how the digital economy is measured. Most online commerce is missing from the platform figures on which companies are valued and sellers are taxed, and millions of the people who sell online are outside those figures altogether. Decisions worth trillions of dollars rest on numbers that show a fraction of the activity they are taken to describe. The ambition of this thesis is to bring the rest into view: to show how much of the online economy the current figures miss, where it is, and who is in it.

Measuring what the current figures miss begins with one question: how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth? The question has two halves. The first concerns size, which is how much of the activity lies outside the figure. The second concerns growth, which is whether the figure still moves with the activity once prices or coverage change. A large wedge would matter less if it stayed in proportion; the second half asks whether it did.

The research question is addressed through three objectives. The first is to measure the invisible wedge across the platform histories available from 2020 to 2025. The second is to reconcile selected large movements with disclosed changes in monetization, customer incentives, revenue recognition and reporting scope. The third is to compare the platform evidence with national e-commerce and payment figures, to test whether they give the same account of economic scale and growth.

The thesis tests two hypotheses. H1 says that a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value. H2 says that national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online, and less from higher sales per business. An entry test then asks whether a rise in the count of online sellers comes from sellers entering for the first time. A further analysis estimates how many sellers a rule built on platform records can reach.

In brief: revenue shows only a small part of the commerce platforms carry, and marketplaces carry only about a fifth of online sales value in BPS's survey. In Indonesia, platform revenue grew faster than the commerce behind it, mainly because platforms kept a larger share of each sale, not because buyers bought more. National growth came mainly from more sellers, as H2 states. But about half of the 2023 rise in the count of sellers is not explained by first-time entry.

## 1.2 Contributions

The thesis makes three contributions, one for each group that relies on these figures: investors, statistics offices, and the authorities that tax and regulate online selling.

For investors, the thesis shows how small a part of the commerce a platform's revenue records. That matters for value, because the market values of internet firms track revenue (Bowen, Davis and Rajgopal 2002). It matters for growth too: the larger the wedge is next to revenue, the more a small change in fees or discounts moves revenue growth. The thesis traces why revenue moved, which lets an investor check whether reported growth came from more commerce or from a larger share of each sale. A prediction registered in advance then tests how market value moves with revenue and with commerce.

For statistics offices, the thesis shows where the commerce outside the platforms sits and tests what a rise in the count of online sellers represents. Most online sellers are small businesses that keep no financial statements. Each reports the year it began selling online, so the survey shows how much of a rise is first-time entry. The test was specified before the survey files were opened.

For tax authorities and regulators, the thesis measures how far rules based on platform records reach. Such a rule reaches only the sellers who use platforms, so where most sellers work outside them, it covers a minority. The thesis also sets each recent rule, from the cap on ride-hailing commissions to the limit on discounts, against the figure it acts on. Several other countries have adopted similar rules, so the same question applies there.

Earlier work has already compared platform transaction values with national e-commerce statistics. This thesis adds connected evidence for one country, drawn from the platforms' accounts, the sellers' own survey responses and the rules that act on both.

## 1.3 Structure of the thesis

The thesis moves from ideas to evidence and then to what follows from it. Chapter 2 reviews why platform revenue differs from the commerce behind it, how online selling is measured, and what platform records can show. It ends with the questions the literature leaves open. Chapter 3 turns those questions into measures, two hypotheses, the entry test and four further analyses. Chapter 4 describes the data and the tests that use them. Chapter 5 gives the results, first for the platforms and then for the national figures. Chapter 6 asks how far Indonesia's recent rules reach and what the findings mean for other countries. Chapter 7 concludes and states the limits of the evidence.
