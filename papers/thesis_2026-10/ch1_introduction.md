# Chapter 1. Introduction

## 1.1 The numbers everyone uses

Nobody watches the online economy directly. We see it through a few numbers, and each number comes from a different place.

Investors read what platforms report about themselves: how much passed through the platform, and how much the platform kept. Statistics offices read surveys of the people who sell online. Tax offices have started to read the records that platforms keep about their sellers. In several countries, governments now ask platforms to report what their sellers earn, or to collect tax from them.

Each of these numbers stands in for something nobody sees whole: how much people buy, and how many people sell. A stand-in is useful while it moves with the thing it stands for. When it stops, two honest numbers can tell opposite stories about the same year. The people relying on them then reach opposite conclusions, and each is right about their own number.

This thesis asks when that happens, and how much the numbers leave out.

## 1.2 Indonesia, 2023

Indonesia is a good place to ask. It is the largest online market in Southeast Asia. Its platforms keep a very thin slice of each sale. Most of its online sellers never use a marketplace. They sell through WhatsApp, Instagram and other chat and social apps.

In 2023 the country argued about online selling in public. Traders at Tanah Abang, a large wholesale market in Jakarta, said cheap goods on TikTok Shop were taking their customers. In late September the government banned shopping inside social media apps. TikTok Shop closed in early October.

The official figures for that year came out later. BPS, the national statistics office, reported that online sales rose 41 percent. It also reported about 820,000 more online sellers than the year before. Company accounts pointed the same way: the revenue of Tokopedia, one of the largest marketplaces, rose 53 percent. Read together, these numbers describe a boom.

Other numbers from the same year do not. The value of what people bought on Tokopedia fell 9 percent. The central bank's figure for online shopping fell about 5 percent. The same year looks like a boom through some numbers and a slowdown through others.

## 1.3 Research question, objectives and hypotheses

One question drives the thesis:

> How much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth?

Two terms carry the question. Transaction value is everything buyers paid through a platform. Revenue is the part the platform keeps. I call the difference the invisible wedge. The term means what a revenue-only measure leaves out. It does not mean unrecorded activity.

The thesis has three objectives, unchanged from the proposal:

1. Measure the invisible wedge across the platform histories available from 2020 to 2025.
2. Reconcile selected large movements with disclosed changes in monetization, customer incentives, revenue recognition and reporting scope.
3. Compare the platform evidence with national e-commerce and payment indicators, to test whether they give the same account of economic scale and growth.

Two hypotheses test the two numbers people lean on most. H1 asks whether a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value. H2 asks whether national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online. Chapter 3 states both in full.

## 1.4 What this thesis finds

Revenue shows a thin slice of the buying. Of every 100 rupiah spent through Indonesia's large platforms, revenue shows about 7. On three platforms in 2023, the rest came to about US$40 billion. The platforms themselves carry only about a fifth of online sales.

Revenue did not grow in proportion to sales, so H1 is rejected. Indonesian platforms changed their prices by about as much as platforms abroad. Their slice is far thinner, so the same change moved their revenue about three times as much. Much of the change came from paying for fewer discounts.

In 2023, buying through the platforms did not boom. Most sellers told BPS their online sales were flat or down. Their main complaint was a lack of customers.

On BPS's published numbers, H2 holds: growth came from more businesses. The survey's own answers change what that means. About half the new sellers were not first-time sellers. The extra count leaned toward older, long-running small businesses selling through WhatsApp and social media.

Indonesia's new rules lean on the same numbers. The marketplace tax can collect from about 1 online seller in 20.

Less had moved online in 2023 than the headline figures suggested.

## 1.5 Contributions

The thesis adds three things.

First, it gives the invisible wedge a job. Earlier work explains why revenue and transaction value differ. This thesis shows why the gap matters for growth. The larger the wedge is next to revenue, the more an ordinary price change moves revenue growth. Evidence from 5 Indonesian and regional platforms and 27 platforms abroad supports this.

Second, it tests what a jump in a national count of online sellers means. Each seller in BPS's survey reports the year they started selling online. That answer shows how much of a jump is first-time entry. The test was written down before the survey files were opened.

Third, it measures how far platform-based rules reach. In a country where most sellers work outside the platforms, a rule built on platform records reaches a minority of them.

Earlier work has already set platform transaction values beside national e-commerce statistics. The contribution here is the connected evidence for one country: the platforms' accounts, the sellers' own answers, and the rules built on both.
