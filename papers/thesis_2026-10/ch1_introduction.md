# Chapter 1. Introduction

Digital commerce has changed how goods and services are bought and sold in Indonesia. Sellers in small towns reach buyers across the country, businesses operate from mobile phones, and payments are made by QR code. The figures used to measure this activity, however, differ on both its size and its growth.

The difference was visible in 2023. Traders at Tanah Abang, a major wholesale market in Jakarta, reported that their customers had moved to sellers on TikTok Shop. The government then banned transactions on social media platforms, and TikTok Shop closed in Indonesia shortly afterwards. The national statistics office, BPS, later reported that online sales rose by about 41 percent that year. The central bank's measure of online shopping fell by about 5 percent over the same period.

The same divergence appears within a single company. Between 2022 and 2023, Tokopedia's revenue rose by 53 percent while the value of transactions on the platform fell by 9 percent. Both figures come from the company's own reports. Measured by revenue, the platform grew; measured by transaction value, it contracted.

These figures differ because they measure different things. A platform that handles 100 units of sales may keep only a small part as its own revenue, in the form of commissions and fees. The rest belongs to the sale itself: the seller's receipts, the driver's fare and other amounts that pass through. An analyst reading the platform's accounts sees the small part, while one measuring the commerce it carries sees the whole. This thesis calls the difference the invisible wedge. The term refers to what a revenue-only measure leaves out, not to activity that goes unrecorded.

The difference matters because money and public decisions depend on these figures, and not only in Indonesia. Businesses in 43 economies sold almost US$27 trillion online in 2022, by the United Nations' estimate, and platforms carry a growing part of that trade. The companies that run them are among the most valuable in the world, and they are valued largely on the growth they report. If part of that growth comes from a platform keeping more of each sale, and not from more commerce, then part of what investors pay for is a pricing decision. Governments face the same uncertainty. They use these figures to judge how large their digital economies are, and a growing number now ask platforms to report what their sellers earn, or to collect tax from them. A rule built on one figure reaches only what that figure covers. Each of these readers is acting on a number without knowing how far it can be relied on.

Existing research gives investors and governments limited help in checking the figures they use. It explains why the figures differ: platforms set prices on both sides of a market, accounting rules decide how much of each sale counts as revenue, and national accounts separate a platform's own service from the commerce it carries. Statistical agencies have also begun to set business surveys beside platform transaction values. What this work does not show is when a figure stops moving with the activity it is used to represent, or by how much. Answering that requires two things that are rarely available together: a platform's revenue and transaction value reported over several years, and a national count of sellers that can be examined from the inside.

Indonesia is one of the few markets where both kinds of evidence are available. Several of its platforms report their transaction value and their revenue each year, and BPS asks online sellers when they began selling and how their sales changed. The stakes are also concrete there. For 2024, the state's two main estimates of online sales are about Rp1,290 trillion and about Rp490 trillion, so the size of the market is uncertain by a factor of more than two. The government is nevertheless writing rules for that market, on platform commissions, on discounts and on tax. Its new levy on marketplace sellers would raise about Rp1 trillion a year on one measure of the market and about Rp5 trillion on another, before exemptions. Indonesia is also a market where platform figures and survey figures are likely to part, because most of its online sellers work outside the marketplaces, through messaging and social media applications. The events of 2023 suggest that they did part.

## 1.1 Research question and objectives

This thesis sets out to show that most online commerce is missing from the platform figures used to value companies and to design tax rules, and to show where that missing commerce is.

For that, one question has to be answered: how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth?

The thesis answers it in three steps. It measures how much a revenue-based figure leaves out. It tests whether platform revenue and the national count of sellers moved with the activity they are used to represent. It estimates how many sellers a rule based on platform records can reach.

Three objectives follow. The first is to measure the invisible wedge across the platform histories available from 2020 to 2025. The second is to reconcile selected large movements with disclosed changes in monetization, customer incentives, revenue recognition and reporting scope. The third is to compare the platform evidence with national e-commerce and payment indicators, to test whether they give the same account of economic scale and growth.

Two hypotheses test the links between these figures. H1 states that a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value. H2 states that national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online, and less from higher sales per business. Chapter 3 sets out both in full.

In brief, the thesis finds that platform revenue and the official count of sellers both stopped moving with the activity behind them in the period studied. Platform revenue rose mainly because platforms kept a larger share of each sale, and about half of the 2023 increase in the official count of sellers was not first-time entry. Chapters 5 and 6 establish how large these effects were and what follows from them.

## 1.2 Contributions

The thesis makes three contributions, one for each group that relies on these figures: investors, statistics offices, and the authorities that tax and regulate online selling.

For those who value platforms, it shows why the size of the invisible wedge matters for growth and not only for scale. The larger the wedge is relative to revenue, the more a given change in fees or discounts moves revenue growth. This gives investors a direct check on reported growth: whether it came from more commerce or from the platform keeping a larger share of each sale.

For those who measure the economy, it provides a test of what an increase in a national count of online sellers represents. Each seller in the survey reports the year they began selling online, so the survey itself shows how much of an increase is first-time entry. The test was specified before the survey files were opened. It bears directly on a market whose official size estimates differ by a factor of more than two.

For those who design tax and regulation, it measures how far rules based on platform records reach. Where most sellers work outside the platforms, such rules cover a minority of them, and the revenue they can raise depends on which measure of the market is correct. Because several countries have adopted similar rules, the result is relevant beyond Indonesia.

Earlier work has already compared platform transaction values with national e-commerce statistics. The contribution of this thesis is the connected evidence for one country, drawn from the platforms' accounts, the sellers' survey responses and the rules built on both.

## 1.3 Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2 reviews the literature on platform revenue, the measurement of online selling and the use of platform records for tax. Chapter 3 defines the measures and develops the hypotheses. Chapter 4 describes the data and the tests. Chapter 5 presents the results, beginning with the platforms and moving to the national figures. Chapter 6 examines how far Indonesia's recent rules reach and what the findings imply for other countries. Chapter 7 concludes and states the limitations.
