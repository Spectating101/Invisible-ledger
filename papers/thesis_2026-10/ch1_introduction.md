# Chapter 1. Introduction

Digital commerce has changed how goods and services are bought and sold in Indonesia. Sellers in small towns reach buyers across the country, businesses operate from mobile phones, and payments are made by QR code. The figures used to measure this activity, however, differ on both its size and its growth.

The difference was visible in 2023. Traders at Tanah Abang, a major wholesale market in Jakarta, reported that their customers had moved to sellers on TikTok Shop. The government then banned transactions on social media platforms, and TikTok Shop closed in Indonesia shortly afterwards. The national statistics office, BPS, later reported that online sales rose by about 41 percent that year. The central bank's measure of online shopping fell by about 5 percent over the same period.

The same divergence appears within a single company. Between 2022 and 2023, Tokopedia's revenue rose by 53 percent while the value of transactions on the platform fell by 9 percent. Both figures come from the company's own reports. Measured by revenue, the platform grew; measured by transaction value, it contracted.

These figures differ because they measure different things. A platform that handles 100 units of sales may keep only a small part as its own revenue, in the form of commissions and fees. The rest belongs to the sale itself: the seller's receipts, the driver's fare and other amounts that pass through. An analyst reading the platform's accounts sees the small part, while one measuring the commerce it carries sees the whole. This thesis calls the difference the invisible wedge. The term refers to what a revenue-only measure leaves out, not to activity that goes unrecorded.

Large sums and public decisions rest on these figures. Investors committed about US$2.6 billion to the public listings of Bukalapak and GoTo in 2021 and 2022. GoTo was valued at about US$28 billion when it listed, and it had lost more than half of that value by the end of the year. The state's own estimates of the market differ just as widely. For 2024, BPS puts Indonesia's online sales at about Rp1,290 trillion and Bank Indonesia at about Rp490 trillion. Indonesia's new tax on marketplace sellers would raise about Rp1 trillion or about Rp5 trillion a year before exemptions, depending on which measure of the market is used. A reader who relies on the wrong figure can pay for growth that did not occur in the underlying commerce, plan revenue on a base several times too large, or regulate a market on a mistaken view of its direction.

Existing research does not protect these readers. It explains why revenue and transaction value differ: platforms set prices on both sides of a market, and accounting rules decide how much of each sale counts as revenue. It also recognises that surveys and platform records cover different sellers. It does not show how large the differences become in practice, or when a figure stops moving with the activity it is used to represent. An investor, a statistician and a tax official are each left without a way to tell when their own figure has stopped being a reliable guide.

Indonesia is a suitable place to examine this, for three reasons. The stakes described above are concentrated there: it is the largest digital market in Southeast Asia, and its government is now writing rules on platform commissions, discounts and tax. The figures can also be checked against what lies beneath them. Several Indonesian platforms report both their transaction value and their revenue, and BPS asks online sellers directly when they began selling and how their sales changed. Finally, most Indonesian online sellers work outside the marketplaces, through messaging and social media applications. A figure built on the platforms and a figure built on a survey of sellers are likely to part in such a market, and the 2023 episode suggests that they did.

The aim of this thesis is to establish how far each of these figures can be relied on, and for what. It measures how much a revenue-based figure leaves out. It tests whether platform revenue and the national count of sellers move with the activity they are used to represent. It then estimates how many sellers a rule based on platform records can reach. The evidence comes from the platforms' reports, a comparison group of 27 listed platforms in other countries, and BPS's survey responses for three years.

## 1.1 Research question and objectives

This aim is expressed in one research question: how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth?

Three objectives follow, unchanged from the proposal. The first is to measure the invisible wedge across the platform histories available from 2020 to 2025. The second is to reconcile selected large movements with disclosed changes in monetization, customer incentives, revenue recognition and reporting scope. The third is to compare the platform evidence with national e-commerce and payment indicators, to test whether they give the same account of economic scale and growth.

Two hypotheses test the links between these figures. H1 states that a platform's revenue grows in proportion to its transaction value. H2 states that national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online, and less from higher sales per business. Chapter 3 sets out both in full.

## 1.2 Contributions

The thesis makes three contributions, one for each group of readers described above.

For those who value platforms, it shows why the size of the invisible wedge matters for growth and not only for scale. The larger the wedge is relative to revenue, the more a given change in fees or discounts moves revenue growth. This gives investors a direct check on reported growth: whether it came from more commerce or from the platform keeping a larger share of each sale. The amounts committed to Indonesian platforms show what depends on that distinction.

For those who measure the economy, it provides a test of what an increase in a national count of online sellers represents. Each seller in the survey reports the year they began selling online, so the survey itself shows how much of an increase is first-time entry. The test was specified before the survey files were opened. It bears directly on a market whose official size estimates differ by a factor of more than two.

For those who design tax and regulation, it measures how far rules based on platform records reach. Where most sellers work outside the platforms, such rules cover a minority of them, and the revenue they can raise depends on which measure of the market is correct. This is relevant beyond Indonesia, because several countries have adopted similar rules.

Earlier work has already compared platform transaction values with national e-commerce statistics. The contribution of this thesis is the connected evidence for one country, drawn from the platforms' accounts, the sellers' survey responses and the rules built on both.

## 1.3 Structure of the thesis

Chapter 2 reviews the literature on platform revenue, the measurement of online selling and the use of platform records for tax. Chapter 3 defines the measures and develops the hypotheses. Chapter 4 describes the data and the tests. Chapter 5 presents the results, beginning with the platforms and moving to the national figures. Chapter 6 examines how far Indonesia's recent rules reach and what the findings imply for other countries. Chapter 7 concludes and states the limitations.
