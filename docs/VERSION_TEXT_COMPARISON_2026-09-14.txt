     « Abstract »
     This study measures the difference between the transaction value digital platforms
     process in Indonesia and the revenue those platforms recognize for themselves. I call
     that difference the invisible wedge and measure it with an Ecosystem Ratio, defined as
     unbooked gross transaction value divided by booked platform revenue. Because no
     Indonesian platform discloses a strictly country-labelled matched pair, every
     observation is retained under a named evidence tier and tiers are never pooled.
     Seventeen candidate platform-year levels across five issuer series spanning FY2019 to
     FY2025 show that the wedge moves, and disclosed revenue components explain the largest
     movements. Forty-eight matched issuer-years outside Indonesia corroborate that the
     boundary is general to platform intermediation while its magnitude depends on business
     model. Official statistics locate the wider activity beyond issuer accounts, and
     Indonesia’s marketplace withholding rules show how the underlying records can enter
     third-party reporting.

     « 1. Research Background and Motivation »
     Digital commercial activity has expanded on a scale that is easy to observe but
     surprisingly difficult to translate into one economic measure. Commerce can appear as
     activity facilitated through platforms, revenue recognized by platform firms, sales
     recorded by businesses, national e-commerce estimates, payment-system transactions, or
     information available to public institutions. These quantities are related to the same
     broader transformation, but they are not the same economic object and need not move
     together.
     That distinction becomes economically important when the measures do not lead to the
     same account of change. When the digital economy grows, what exactly is growing, and
     where does that growth actually show up? Transaction activity, company performance,
     business participation, and broader economic measures capture different parts of that
     process and can move differently. A narrow focus on any one record can therefore
     misstate where digital transformation is occurring or how its scale should be
     interpreted. This measurement problem is particularly relevant to digitally mediated
     activity that crosses organizational boundaries, where economic activity may be
     distributed across platforms, independent participants, payment systems, and other
     records rather than contained within a single firm.
     This tension motivates the central question of the thesis: If digital commercial
     activity has become so large, where does that growth actually show up in the economy,
     and why can its apparent impact differ depending on how it is measured?
     The platform transaction-revenue boundary provides a natural place to begin.
     Multisided-platform theory explains why platforms may coordinate large volumes of
     third-party commerce while recognizing commissions, fees, advertising, logistics,
     travel, financial-service, or other revenue within a narrower accounting perimeter
     (Rochet & Tirole, 2003; Armstrong, 2006; Hagiu & Wright, 2015). The existence of a gap
     is therefore not itself anomalous. The more informative question is whether
     transaction activity and platform revenue provide the same longitudinal account of
     growth and, when they diverge, what mechanisms explain the difference.
     That longitudinal comparison provides the first empirical step. Once the company-level
     relationship is understood, a further question follows: where does the broader digital
     transformation appear outside issuer accounts, and when do the resulting digital
     traces become useful to statistical or administrative institutions?
     Following that sequence requires a setting in which several independent records can be
     observed without treating them as interchangeable measures. Indonesia provides such a
     setting through issuer disclosures, official BPS e-commerce evidence, Bank Indonesia
     payment statistics, and an evolving platform-reporting architecture. The thesis
     therefore follows digital-commercial activity across these records to determine where
     the economic interpretation changes and what can be learned by reconciling those
     changes.

     « 2. Research Questions and Objectives »
     The analysis follows this sequence through three linked research questions. RQ1
     examines the firm-level boundary between facilitated activity and recognized revenue.
     RQ2 asks what the firm-level evidence leaves out by moving to business participation,
     sales channels, and payments. RQ3 then examines the institutional boundary between the
     existence of digital traces and their eventual statistical or administrative use.
     Together, the three questions aim to identify when the observational record changes
     the economic conclusion, explain material disagreements where the sources allow,
     locate broader digital growth in participation and channel structure, and distinguish
     private traceability from institutionally usable information.
     « 5. Data and Research Design »
     Because each stage of the research question observes a different unit, the empirical
     design remains modular. The modules are linked by the sequence of the argument, but
     their observations are never added into one sample N or treated as if they measured
     the same object.
     « 6. Preliminary Evidence and Feasibility »
     The modules already yield preliminary evidence along the same sequence as the research
     questions. The results first establish that issuer growth signals can diverge, then
     examine why, and finally move outward to broader business, payment, and institutional
     evidence. The final main-sample boundary remains subject to advisor approval.

     « 1.1 Contributions »
     This study makes four contributions. First, it constructs a 2023 Indonesia-focused
     platform estimate and uses the combined 5.88% take rate only as a transparent
     calibration for four additional ASEAN markets. Derived and Group-level inputs are

     policy benchmark, reporting both the policy's observed collections and a two-country
     before-and-after comparison with Vietnam without a causal claim. Third, I rebuild
     GoTo's 2020-2023 Group series from primary filings. The unbooked share remains between
     97.6% and 98.9%, showing a persistent transaction-revenue gap rather than a monotonic
     increase.

     large transaction-to-revenue gaps persist within platforms, and a 47-event
     earnings-announcement panel to test short-window investor response. The event study
     finds no stable association between the ecosystem ratio and market-adjusted cumulative
     abnormal returns.
     « 3. Theoretical framework: the ecosystem ratio »
     To capture the invisible wedge in Indonesia, I propose and construct a proxy ….
     For example, if a platform processes 100 units of transaction value and books 10 units
     as revenue, 90 units pass through the ecosystem without being recorded as the

     10 = 9.
     Ecosystem ratio = (GTV - platform revenue) / platform revenue (1)
     Gross transaction value (GTV), or gross merchandise value (GMV) in some platform
     disclosures, is the total value of transactions processed through a platform,
     regardless of who ultimately receives the proceeds. This construction is consistent
     with the platform-economics literature showing that booked platform revenue need not
     track the total transaction value coordinated through a multisided platform (Rochet
     and Tirole 2003; Evans and Schmalensee 2016).
     The ecosystem ratio describes the scale of participant-facing transaction value
     relative to the platform's auditable revenue base. A higher ratio indicates a larger
     unbooked transaction flow relative to the platform's own revenue; it does not by
     itself imply greater participant profit, taxable income, value-added, non-compliance,
     or tax due.
     This distinction is essential. Platform filings and investor disclosures can make the
     accounting gap measurable, but the tax treatment of the residual varies according to
     participant type, deductible expenses, tax thresholds, registration status, and taxes
     already remitted through other channels. The empirical contribution of my paper is to
     quantify the scale of the recorded transaction flow that is not directly captured by
     the existing tax system.

     « 2.1 Platform economics and the location of value »
     The economics of multisided platforms begins with the interaction of distinct user
     groups whose participation affects the value received by the other side. Rochet and
     Tirole (2003), Caillaud and Jullien (2003), Parker and Van Alstyne (2005), and
     Armstrong (2006) show why platform pricing cannot be read as a simple markup over
     cost: prices and commissions are chosen to balance cross-group network effects,
     participation, and competition. Hagiu and Wright (2015) further distinguish a
     multisided platform from a vertically integrated firm by locating decision rights and
     entrepreneurial effort with affiliated participants rather than inside the platform.
     Evans and Schmalensee (2016) translate the same point into the business-model
     literature. These results explain why low retained shares can coexist with large
     ecosystems and why platform revenue is a poor proxy for the transaction value
     coordinated through the platform.

     The literature therefore suggests a sequence rather than a set of competing ledgers. A
     digital commercial event may appear first as facilitated activity, then as a corporate
     accounting entry, a merchant record, a payment-system trace, a statistical
     observation, or an administrative record. These representations are not expected to be
     identical because each is produced for a different purpose. The analytical question is
     whether using one in place of another changes the economic conclusion.
     A cross-record difference matters when one measure is being used to stand in for a
     broader process, another legitimate measure points to a materially different
     interpretation, and understanding the difference requires reconciling scope or
     mechanism. The thesis therefore treats disagreement as informative only when it
     affects a substantive conclusion about growth, participation, channel structure, or
     institutional visibility.

     economic account that becomes recoverable when the relationships, disagreements, and
     boundaries among partial records are reconciled.

     concept extends across the full sequence of issuer, business, payment, statistical,
     and administrative records. Although the empirical analysis remains focused on
     Indonesian platform commerce, the same measurement logic can arise in adjacent
     digitally mediated activity, including gig work and creator or influencer commerce,
     where economic activity crosses organizational boundaries and no single corporate,
     participant, payment, or statistical record captures the complete process. The
     analytical objective is to determine what each record captures, where their
     interpretations diverge, and what becomes visible through reconciliation.

     the gross receipts earned by merchants, drivers, and other participants may remain
     outside routine third-party income reporting, even though the platform holds a record
     of the transaction that produced them. The wedge does not measure that reporting gap,
     and this paper does not claim it does. It measures the accounting boundary; whether
     the activity behind that boundary is separately recorded and transmitted to public
     institutions is a further question the study then examines.
     « 1.2 Contributions »
     First, the study measures the wedge longitudinally. Earlier evidence for this market
     rests on a single fiscal year; the inventory assembled here spans seventeen retained
     candidate platform-years from FY2019 to FY2025, so the wedge's direction of travel can
     be reported and not only its scale.
     Second, it explains why the wedge moves, reconciling the largest divergences against
     the revenue components issuers themselves disclose — monetization, customer
     incentives, gross-versus-net recognition, and reporting perimeter.
     Third, it establishes whether the boundary is peculiar to Indonesia. Forty-eight
     matched issuer-years across eight non-Indonesian platform businesses provide external
     corroboration and turn the wide cross-platform spread in measured ratios from an
     obstacle into a result.
     Fourth, it connects the accounting boundary to participant records and tax
     administration, using BPS evidence and Indonesia's PMK 37/2025 marketplace withholding
     architecture.
     « 2.2 Revenue recognition and the accounting boundary »
     Accounting determines how much facilitated commerce becomes revenue. Under IFRS 15,
     principal–agent treatment governs gross-versus-net presentation, while customer
     incentives, advertising and service income, acquisitions, and reporting perimeter can
     move revenue without an equivalent change in commerce. De Franco, Kothari and Verdi
     (2011) show why comparability matters to users of financial statements.

     « 2.3 Third-party reporting as tax capacity »
     A central result in public finance is that tax compliance depends strongly on
     third-party information and remittance structure. Kleven et al. (2011) find an evasion
     rate of 0.3 percent on income subject to third-party reporting versus 37 percent on
     self-reported income in Denmark. Pomeranz (2015) shows that the VAT paper trail
     creates self-enforcement across firms, while Naritomi (2019) demonstrates that
     consumer-held records can raise reported sales. Kleven, Kreiner and Saez (2016)
     formalise firms as fiscal intermediaries, and Slemrod (2019) places information
     reporting and remittance regimes among the core instruments of modern enforcement.
     Jensen (2022) connects this mechanism to development: as employment moves into
     organisations that keep records and report income, the feasible tax base expands.
     Platform work creates a new configuration - a large intermediary with detailed records
     but, in many jurisdictions, no comprehensive obligation to report seller or worker
     income. Collins et al. (2019) and Garin et al. (2023) show how platform information
     returns affect the measured prevalence of gig work in the United States. The
     administrative problem is therefore not a lack of data generation; it is the absence,
     incompleteness, or fragmentation of the reporting rule.
     Two finance studies extend this administrative-data channel. Barrios, Hochberg, and Yi
     (2022) show that gig-platform entry increases new business formation and
     small-business lending, while Denes, Lagaras, and Tsoutsoura (2025) use U.S.
     tax-return microdata to show that gig work can serve as a pathway into
     entrepreneurship. Together, these studies reinforce the idea that platform-mediated
     work leaves economically meaningful financial and administrative records.

     « 4. Conceptual Framework: Economic Translation and the Invisible Ledger »
     The literature therefore suggests a sequence rather than a set of competing ledgers. A
     digital commercial event may appear first as facilitated activity, then as a corporate
     accounting entry, a merchant record, a payment-system trace, a statistical
     observation, or an administrative record. These representations are not expected to be
     identical because each is produced for a different purpose. The analytical question is
     whether using one in place of another changes the economic conclusion.
     A cross-record difference matters when one measure is being used to stand in for a
     broader process, another legitimate measure points to a materially different
     interpretation, and understanding the difference requires reconciling scope or
     mechanism. The thesis therefore treats disagreement as informative only when it
     affects a substantive conclusion about growth, participation, channel structure, or
     institutional visibility.
     This is where the term Invisible Ledger becomes useful. It refers to the fuller
     economic account that becomes recoverable when the relationships, disagreements, and
     boundaries among partial records are reconciled.
     The transaction-revenue relationship provides the first empirical clue, but the
     concept extends across the full sequence of issuer, business, payment, statistical,
     and administrative records. Although the empirical analysis remains focused on
     Indonesian platform commerce, the same measurement logic can arise in adjacent
     digitally mediated activity, including gig work and creator or influencer commerce,
     where economic activity crosses organizational boundaries and no single corporate,
     participant, payment, or statistical record captures the complete process. The
     analytical objective is to determine what each record captures, where their
     interpretations diverge, and what becomes visible through reconciliation.

     « 2.3 Third-party reporting as tax capacity »
     A central result in public finance is that tax compliance depends strongly on
     third-party information and remittance structure. Kleven et al. (2011) find an evasion
     rate of 0.3 percent on income subject to third-party reporting versus 37 percent on
     self-reported income in Denmark. Pomeranz (2015) shows that the VAT paper trail
     creates self-enforcement across firms, while Naritomi (2019) demonstrates that
     consumer-held records can raise reported sales. Kleven, Kreiner and Saez (2016)
     formalise firms as fiscal intermediaries, and Slemrod (2019) places information
     reporting and remittance regimes among the core instruments of modern enforcement.
     Jensen (2022) connects this mechanism to development: as employment moves into
     organisations that keep records and report income, the feasible tax base expands.
     Platform work creates a new configuration - a large intermediary with detailed records
     but, in many jurisdictions, no comprehensive obligation to report seller or worker
     income. Collins et al. (2019) and Garin et al. (2023) show how platform information
     returns affect the measured prevalence of gig work in the United States. The
     administrative problem is therefore not a lack of data generation; it is the absence,
     incompleteness, or fragmentation of the reporting rule.
     Two finance studies extend this administrative-data channel. Barrios, Hochberg, and Yi
     (2022) show that gig-platform entry increases new business formation and
     small-business lending, while Denes, Lagaras, and Tsoutsoura (2025) use U.S.
     tax-return microdata to show that gig work can serve as a pathway into
     entrepreneurship. Together, these studies reinforce the idea that platform-mediated
     work leaves economically meaningful financial and administrative records.

     « 3.3 Measuring the digital economy »
     Digital-economy measurement literature addresses that broader boundary. Official
     frameworks distinguish the underlying buyer-seller transaction from the intermediation
     service provided by the platform, while national-accounting work rejects the shortcut
     of treating gross transaction value as value added or an alternative GDP measure.
     Digitalization can nevertheless create measurement difficulties involving
     classification, business perimeter, cross-border activity, prices, private-source
     data, and publication revision (Ahmad & Schreyer, 2016; IMF, 2018; OECD, 2023). These
     frameworks clarify what aggregate measures mean, but they still say little about how
     participating businesses differ in their own records and payment practices.
     « 5. Data and Research Design »
     Because each stage of the research question observes a different unit, the empirical
     design remains modular. The modules are linked by the sequence of the argument, but
     their observations are never added into one sample N or treated as if they measured
     the same object.
     « 5.1 Issuer admission rules »
     An issuer-period is eligible only when transaction and revenue measures cover the same
     period; geography and business scope can be evaluated; currency and units are known;
     original, revised, and pro-forma bases are labelled; definitions are recorded; derived
     values have transparent source inputs; and material structural breaks are flagged.
     Annual totals, quarters, publication vintages, and source inputs are not treated as
     independent observations.
     The direct candidate inventory contains 13 economic periods: seven Blibli 3P periods
     (FY2019-FY2025), four Bukalapak Group periods (FY2020-FY2023), and two Tokopedia
     e-commerce periods (FY2022-FY2023). Tokopedia FY2021 is excluded for period mismatch,
     and Bukalapak FY2024 is excluded because transaction value and revenue cover different
     durations. Grab and Shopee country reconstructions remain conditional sensitivity
     evidence rather than direct core observations.
     « 6.4 Payment traces and administrative records add separate views »
     Bank Indonesia provides that independent payment-system view. Between 2023 and 2024,
     BPS e-commerce value grows 17.08%, while electronic-money shopping, mobile-banking
     payment/purchase, and QRIS transaction values grow at substantially different rates.
     The contrast is expected because payments and sales are different economic objects.
     Its value is therefore not to validate BPS or issuer data, but to show that abundant
     digital traces can expand on a different trajectory from the commerce they help
     record.
     Once trace abundance is separated from economic meaning, the remaining question is
     institutional: when can those traces actually be used by public administration? PMK
     37/2025 shows a legal bridge built around seller identity, transaction-linked
     turnover, withholding, and reporting rather than platform corporate revenue. Current
     evidence establishes that legal architecture, but the implementation postponement
     prevents claims about successful operational matching, compliance, or revenue effects.
