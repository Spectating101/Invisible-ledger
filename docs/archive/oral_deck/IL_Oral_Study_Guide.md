# The Invisible Ledger: study guide in three perspectives (deck v4.10)

Each slide is seen three ways:
- **SLIDE:** what is on the screen, in the thesis's own terms.
- **THESIS:** what the full thesis adds or will expand later.
- **PLAIN:** the same thing in the simplest possible words, with no step skipped and no number dropped. This is for understanding, not a script.
- **PICTURE:** one running analogy, a night market. The owner is the platform, stall till receipts are transaction value, the owner's cut is revenue, the vouchers he gives shoppers are incentives, the city survey is BPS, and vendors selling via WhatsApp outside the gates are the other channels.

**TRAP** marks wording that gets people into trouble.

---

## I. THE PROBLEM

### 1 · Title
- **SLIDE:** The Invisible Ledger: Quantifying the Invisible Wedge in Indonesia's Platform Economy.
- **THESIS:** The "invisible wedge" is what a revenue-only measure leaves out. It is not hidden or unrecorded activity; the thesis outline says so explicitly.
- **PLAIN:** A shop app lets people sell things. Lots of money passes through the app, but the app only keeps a small piece as its own income. If you only look at the app's income, you don't see most of the selling. That unseen part is "the wedge". It isn't secret or illegal. It's just not in that number.
- **PICTURE:** A night market where every stall pays the owner a cut. The owner's book shows only his cuts. All the cash that changed hands at the stalls is the wedge. It isn't hidden; it just isn't in his book.
- **TRAP:** "Invisible" must never sound like hidden money or tax evasion.

### 2 · Hook
- **SLIDE:** Digital commerce changed how a country buys and sells, yet some numbers make it look far smaller than others do. Every report is correct on its own terms.
- **THESIS:** The context chapter covers phones, social selling and QR payments.
- **PLAIN:** Many groups count online selling: the apps, the government, the banks. Each counts a different thing, so each gets a different total. Nobody is lying. They're just not counting the same thing.
- **PICTURE:** Three people describe the same night market. The owner reports his cuts. The city reports what vendors say they sold in its survey. The bank reports QR payments. That gives three honest, different totals.

### 3 · Case: Tanah Abang, 2023
- **SLIDE:**
  - Southeast Asia's largest wholesale market lost customers to sellers live-streaming on phones.
  - The government restricted social-media selling, and TikTok Shop closed.
  - TikTok returned by buying control of Tokopedia.
- **THESIS:** A motivating case only; the study does not evaluate the policy. The TikTok–Tokopedia deal (completed 31 Jan 2024) is why Tokopedia's own series ends in 2023.
- **PLAIN:** A huge old market in Jakarta got quiet because buyers moved to phone live-stream sellers. The government stopped selling through social media, so TikTok Shop closed. TikTok came back by buying a big part of Tokopedia, a large shop app, and putting its shop inside it. Everyone saw buying move to phones. Nobody could say how much, because each number saw only a piece of it.
- **PICTURE:** The old covered market empties because shoppers now buy from vendors live-streaming from their living rooms. The city bans selling through the streaming app, and the streamers' shop closes. The streaming company then buys the biggest night market and moves its stalls inside. Everyone sees the crowd moving, but nobody's count matches anyone else's.

### 4 · One Economy, Several Numbers
- **SLIDE:**
  - **Transaction value (GMV/GTV):** all sales through a platform.
  - **Revenue:** what the platform recognises as its own; investors value platforms mostly on this.
  - **BPS:** what surveyed businesses report selling online, including through social media and messaging.
  - A size gap is normal; none of the numbers is wrong.
  - Abroad, growth can split too (Etsy, eBay, Zalando). Revenue grew faster in 22 of 29 comparisons and moved in the opposite direction in 4, but the drift is usually mild.
- **THESIS:**
  - **How much platforms keep varies:** Shopify about 3%, Mercado Libre about 25%, Zalando about 70% (Zalando also sells its own stock).
  - **Accounting rules decide what counts as revenue:** under IFRS 15, it depends on whether the platform is the seller (principal) or the middleman (agent).
  - **A longer benchmark** comes later.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **Three numbers:** (1) all the money that passes through the app; (2) the part the app keeps, its income; (3) what shops tell the government they sold online, including shops that sell only through chat or social media.
  - **Why the gap is normal:** if an app keeps 10 of every 100, its income is always much smaller than the selling. That's fine.
  - **The real question:** when selling goes up, does the app's income go up by the same amount? In other countries, mostly yes, give or take a little.
- **PICTURE:** Three records of the same night market:
  - **Stall till receipts:** everything sold (transaction value).
  - **The owner's book:** his cut (revenue).
  - **The city's survey:** what vendors say they sold, including vendors selling from WhatsApp outside the gates (BPS).

  An owner keeping 10 of every 100 is normal. The question is whether, when the stalls sell 20% more, the owner's book also grows about 20%. In foreign markets it mostly does, give or take a little.

### 5 · Why Indonesia, Why Tokopedia
- **SLIDE:**
  - Indonesia is the largest digital market in Southeast Asia, and commerce moved online fast through apps, social media and chat.
  - Testable because local firms report both numbers over time, and BPS splits national sales by channel and by number of sellers.
  - Tokopedia is roughly Indonesia's eBay, part of GoTo. Puzzle 2022→23: revenue **+53.2%**, transaction value **−8.9%**, both from GoTo's own disclosures.
- **THESIS:** Southeast Asia's digital economy grew from about US$100B (2020) to US$263B (2024), per Google, Temasek & Bain. Shopee and Tokopedia held about 70% of estimated marketplace transaction value in 2023, per Momentum Works.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **Why Indonesia:** biggest online market in the region, and it moved fast.
  - **Why it can be tested:** a few Indonesian apps publish both the "money through" number and the "money kept" number every year, so you can compare each app with itself. The government also publishes how many shops sell online and where.
  - **The puzzle:** in one year Tokopedia's income went up by half while the money passing through it went down by almost a tenth. Look at income and it's booming; look at selling and it's shrinking.
- **PICTURE:** The biggest night market in the region. Its owner's book is up 53% on last year, while the stall receipts are down 9%. Same market, same year, opposite stories.

### 6 · A Bigger Slice of a Smaller Pie
- **SLIDE:**
  - Transaction value fell, net revenue rose, and incentives (discounts, vouchers, promotions) fell.
  - **60.6%** of the net-revenue increase reconciles to lower incentives and 39.4% to higher gross revenue. This is arithmetic reconciliation, not causal.
  - Investors read revenue, statisticians read surveys, and tax offices draw on marketplace records. The same year can point in opposite directions.
- **THESIS:**
  - **The numbers:** gross revenue Rp8.14T → Rp8.99T; incentives Rp4.11T → Rp2.81T; net revenue Rp4.03T → Rp6.18T (GoTo 2024 annual report).
  - **What comes later:** the thesis diagnoses the other big divergences (Blibli, Bukalapak) the same way, using disclosed incentives, fees and reporting scope.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **How the income is counted:** the app counts its fees, then subtracts the discounts and vouchers it gave to buyers. What's left is its reported income.
  - **What happened in 2023:** Tokopedia gave far fewer discounts (4.11 trillion down to 2.81 trillion rupiah), so less was subtracted. Income rose from 4.03 to 6.18 trillion, even though less selling went through the app.
  - **Where the increase came from:** about 6 of every 10 rupiah of the increase came from cutting discounts; about 4 came from higher fees.
  - **The pie picture:** the pie (selling) got smaller, but the app took a bigger slice.
  - **Why it matters:** an investor sees growth, while someone counting the economy sees shrinking. Same app, same year.
  - **What it's not:** the adding-up shows where the change sits in the accounts, not why management did it.
- **PICTURE:** **Last year:** the owner handed shoppers "Rp10,000 off" vouchers and paid for them out of his cut. His book showed his cut minus the vouchers.

  **This year:** he handed out far fewer vouchers. Stall receipts went down, but his book went up, because much less was being subtracted.

  **The split:** of every 10 rupiah his book gained, about 6 came from fewer vouchers and about 4 from higher fees. An investor reading his book sees a booming market; the city counting sales sees a shrinking one.

  The arithmetic shows *where* the change sits, not *why* he cut vouchers.
- **TRAP:** Say "transaction value fell", never "fewer transactions"; the count of sales isn't observed. Never say incentives *caused* anything.

## II. THE QUESTION

### 7 · Literature
- **SLIDE:**
  - **Five fields:** platform economics (Rochet & Tirole; Hagiu & Wright), accounting (IFRS 15; Bowen, Davis & Rajgopal), KPI research (Givoly et al.), measurement (Henderson, Storeygard & Weil; Ahmad & Schreyer), digital records (Berg et al.; OECD).
  - **Missing:** when revenue stops representing commerce; whether platform data represents national growth; how large and persistent the differences are.
  - **Why it's hard:** it needs both numbers from the same firm over time, plus a benchmark.
  - **The study's place:** it directly tests stability and links it to national measurement.
- **THESIS:** The full literature chapter.
- **PLAIN:** Each field explains a piece:
  - Apps set prices on both sides, charging sellers and subsidising buyers, so income needn't follow selling.
  - Accounting rules decide what counts as income.
  - A company number becomes less useful when the way it's calculated changes.
  - A stand-in number must be checked against the thing it stands in for.
  - App records can show activity that income misses.

  Nobody has directly checked, over time and against other countries, when using income in place of selling gives the wrong growth answer, and linked that to the government's national numbers. That's this study's spot.
- **PICTURE:** Five specialists each explain part of the puzzle:
  - **The pricing expert:** the owner charges stalls and subsidises shoppers, so his cut needn't follow sales.
  - **The accountant:** what counts as the owner's income depends on whether he is the seller or just the landlord.
  - **The KPI researcher:** a number changes meaning when the way it's calculated changes.
  - **The statistician:** a stand-in number must be checked against the real thing.
  - **The tax officer:** the owner's records can show stall activity his income leaves out.

  Nobody has checked, over years and against other markets, when reading the owner's book gives the wrong growth answer for the whole market, and tied that to the city's survey.
- **TRAP:** Say "directly tests", never "nobody has done this".

### 8 · Research Question and Hypotheses
- **SLIDE:**
  - **RQ:** how much economic activity remains invisible when platforms are measured through revenue, and when does that choice change the growth conclusion?
  - **H1:** transaction value and revenue grow proportionally. The share kept moves with fees, incentives and accounting.
  - **H2:** national e-commerce growth comes mainly from more participating businesses, not higher sales per business (Hummels & Klenow, 2005). Such growth can sit in channels platforms don't cover.
  - **Supporting analyses:** growth by channel; whether marketplace sellers keep better records.
  - **The circles:** revenue sits inside platform commerce, which sits inside all online commerce.
- **THESIS:** A later market test: does the stock market tell the two kinds of revenue growth apart?
- **PLAIN:** Picture three circles, one inside another. The small one is the app's income. The middle one is all selling through the app. The big one is all online selling in the country. The study asks two questions:
  - **Link 1 (H1):** does the small circle grow in step with the middle one? The default assumption is yes.
  - **Link 2 (H2):** when the big circle grows, is it because more shops start selling online, or because each shop sells more? If it's mostly more shops, and many sell through chat and social media, the app numbers can miss that growth.
- **PICTURE:** Three circles, one inside another: the owner's book, inside all stall sales in his market, inside all selling in the city (street vendors, WhatsApp sellers, live-streamers).
  - **H1:** does the owner's book grow in step with stall sales?
  - **H2:** when the city's selling grows, is it because more vendors start selling or because each vendor sells more? If it's more vendors, and many sell outside the market gates, the market's numbers miss that growth.

### 9 · From Question to Measurement
- **SLIDE:**
  - Revenue = share kept × transaction value.
  - **W = V − R:** the wedge, in money.
  - **E = W/R = 1/m − 1:** how much commerce sits outside revenue per unit kept.
  - **D = g(R) − g(V):** the central test. D ≈ 0 means revenue still tracks commerce growth; a large |D| means the share kept changed.
- **THESIS:**
  - **Exact relation:** (1+g_R) = (1+g_m)(1+g_V), so D = g_m(1+g_V). D reflects a change in the share kept; D ≈ g_m only for small changes.
  - **Reading E:** E = 9 means nine units outside revenue for each one kept, about 10% kept.
  - **Exchange rates don't matter:** growth is computed in each firm's own currency.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **Income = the slice the app keeps × all the selling.**
  - **W** is how much money the app didn't keep.
  - **E** is how many rupiah of selling there are for each rupiah the app keeps. If the app keeps 10%, E is 9.
  - **Why W and E aren't the test:** they only describe size, and size is supposed to be big. The test is D: how much faster income grew than selling. If the slice stays the same, income and selling grow at the same speed, so D is about zero and you can trust income to tell you about selling. If the slice changes, D gets big, and income tells you a different story. So D is basically "did the slice change?"
- **PICTURE:** Owner's book = cut % × stall sales.
  - **W:** the stall cash not in his book.
  - **E:** rupiah of stall sales per rupiah he keeps. A 10% cut means E = 9.
  - **D:** how much faster his book grew than stall sales.

  **If the cut % never changes**, the two grow together and D ≈ 0.

  **Worked example:** stall sales stay flat, but he raises his cut from 10% to 12%. His book grows 20% while sales grow 0%, so D = 20 points, all from the cut changing. That is why D is the test: it catches the cut moving. E only tells you how big the cut is, and a big E is normal.

## III. THE EVIDENCE

### 10 · Dataset
- **SLIDE:**
  - **Indonesian firms:** only three report both numbers themselves (Tokopedia, Blibli, Bukalapak), giving 11 platform-years and **8 year-to-year comparisons** (2020–2025).
  - **Benchmark:** 8 listed platforms abroad, 29 comparisons.
  - **Other sources:** BPS surveys 2022–24 for H2; Grab and Shopee are constructed figures, used for 2023 scale only; Bank Indonesia payments are context only.
  - **Matching rule:** same period and, as closely as possible, the same business activity; mismatches excluded, issuer labels (GTV, GMV, TPV) checked.
  - **Design:** each firm against itself, a benchmark abroad, no single case driving results.
  - **Kept apart:** reported vs constructed figures; platform vs national data, never pooled.
  - Lazada and TikTok Shop are excluded; Tokopedia's series ends in 2023.
- **THESIS:** Quarterly pairs to thicken H1, and a longer benchmark.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **The sample is small because few publish both numbers.** Only three Indonesian apps publish both. Across the years that gives 8 "this year versus last year" comparisons.
  - **What "normal" looks like:** 8 foreign apps give 29 comparisons.
  - **The rules:** each app is compared only with itself; the two numbers must cover the same months and the same business; estimated numbers are never mixed with reported ones; app numbers are never mixed with government numbers.
  - Lazada and TikTok Shop don't publish Indonesian income, so they're out.
- **PICTURE:** Only three night markets publish both the owner's book and the stall totals. So each market is compared only with its own previous year, never market against market. Eight foreign markets supply 29 year-on-year pairs that show what "normal drift" looks like.

  The rules: both numbers must cover the same months and the same stalls; estimated books are never mixed with published ones; owner numbers are never mixed with the city's survey.

### 11 · Scale
- **SLIDE:** 2023, in US$ billions:

  | Platform | V | R | W | E | Source |
  |---|---|---|---|---|---|
  | Tokopedia | 16.33 | 0.41 | 15.93 | 39.3 | reported |
  | Shopee | 21.52 | 2.15 | 19.37 | 9.0 | constructed |
  | Grab | 5.38 | 0.61 | 4.78 | 7.9 | constructed |
  | **Total** | **43.23** | **3.16** | **40.07** | **12.7** | |

  V/R ≈ 13.7. A large, stable wedge would not by itself mislead about growth; D tests that.
- **THESIS:**
  - **Shopee's construction:** an assumed 9–11% take rate gives a total of US$39.9–40.3B, and dropping any one platform leaves at least US$20.7B.
  - **Cross-check:** Blibli's E is 43.4 and Bukalapak's 36.0, close to Tokopedia's 39.3. Shopee's and Grab's ratios are company-wide.
- **PLAIN:** Across three apps in 2023, about 43 billion dollars of selling went through them, but they kept only about 3 billion. That's about 13.7 dollars of selling per dollar kept (or 12.7 dollars *outside* income per dollar kept, which is the same thing minus one). Big, but expected. A big gap that stays the same size doesn't break anything. Only a gap that changes breaks the growth story, which is what comes next.
- **PICTURE:** Across three markets in 2023, about 43 billion dollars of stall sales went through, and the owners kept about 3 billion. The pile of till receipts is 13 to 14 times the owners' books. A big pile is expected. The question is whether that ratio stays put from year to year.
- **TRAP:** 13.7 is V/R and 12.7 is E = V/R − 1. Both are correct.

### 12 · H1: The Pattern
- **SLIDE:** A chart of D for each platform-year. Revenue grew faster in 6 of 8; median gap **38.92 pp**. Two reversals, where revenue rose while transaction value fell: Tokopedia 2022–23 and Blibli 2024–25.
- **THESIS:**
  - **Every bar:** Tokopedia 22–23 +62.10; Bukalapak 22–23 +15.74, 21–22 +68.21, 20–21 −5.84; Blibli 24–25 +13.95, 23–24 +14.41, 22–23 +430.61 (axis broken), 21–22 −72.18.
  - **Blibli 24–25 in detail:** transaction value −1.89%, revenue +12.07%.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **What a bar is:** income growth minus selling growth, for one app in one year. A bar to the right means income grew faster.
  - **The count:** 6 of 8 bars point right. In 2 of those 6, selling actually shrank while income grew. The other 2 bars point left: Bukalapak 2020–21 and Blibli 2021–22.
  - **The size:** the middle gap is about 39 percentage points, which is big. But numbers never match perfectly, so is this unusual? That's the next slide.
- **PICTURE:** For each market-year, draw one bar: owner's book growth minus stall-sales growth. A bar to the right means the book grew faster.
  - **6 of 8 bars go right.** In 2 of those 6, stall sales actually fell while the book rose.
  - **2 bars go left.**
  - **The typical bar** is about 39 points long.
- **TRAP:** The 2 reversals are inside the 6. Never say "6 one way, 2 the other".

### 13 · H1: Formal Evidence
- **SLIDE:**

  | | Indonesia | Abroad |
  |---|---|---|
  | Median \|D\| | 38.92 pp | 7.10 pp |
  | Growth correlation | 0.10 | 0.98 (excl. Sea) |
  | Comparisons | 8 | 29 |

  - One-sided Mann–Whitney: **p = 0.002**.
  - Robustness: drop the largest case, 0.006; exclude Sea, <0.001; one median per firm, 0.024.
  - The difference isn't direction but size and weak co-movement. Evidence inconsistent with H1.
- **THESIS:** Quarterly pairs and a longer benchmark add power.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **The comparison:** the typical gap abroad is about 7 points; in Indonesia it's about 39, more than five times bigger.
  - **Moving together:** abroad, income and selling move almost perfectly together (0.98). In Indonesia they barely do (0.10).
  - **The test:** the Mann–Whitney test lines up all the gaps from smallest to biggest and checks whether Indonesia's sit at the big end more than chance allows. It uses ranks, not raw sizes, because there are few numbers and some are extreme (430). It's one-sided because "Indonesia's gaps are bigger" was predicted before looking.
  - **The result:** p = 0.002 means that if Indonesia were really like abroad, a pattern this extreme would show up about 2 times in 1,000.
  - **Robustness:** it still holds after removing the biggest case, after removing Sea (whose Shopee also operates in Indonesia, so it isn't truly "abroad"), and when each firm counts only once, since years from the same firm aren't independent.
  - **Direction isn't the finding:** abroad, income also tends to grow a bit faster. What's different in Indonesia is how big the gap is and how loosely the two move.
- **PICTURE:** **Length:** foreign markets' bars are typically about 7 points long; Indonesia's about 39.

  **The ranking contest (Mann–Whitney):** line up all 37 bars by length (8 Indonesian plus 29 foreign). If Indonesia were no different, its 8 bars would be scattered randomly through the line. They bunch at the long end so strongly that luck would produce this only about 2 times in 1,000.

  **The checks:** it still holds when you remove the longest bar, when you drop the foreign market that also runs stalls in Indonesia (Sea), and when each market counts only once.

  **Moving together:** abroad, book and sales move like two dancers in step (0.98). In Indonesia they barely move together (0.10).
- **TRAP:** Say "inconsistent with H1", never "proves".

### 14 · H2: National Evidence
- **SLIDE:**

  | BPS | 2022–23 | 2023–24 |
  |---|---|---|
  | E-commerce value | +40.60% | +17.08% |
  | Businesses | +27.40% | +15.31% |
  | Sales per business | +10.36% | +1.54% |
  | Share from more businesses | ≈71% | ≈90% |

  - **Channel callout:** marketplace category +1.45%, other channels +20.57%, so 98.46% of measured 2023–24 growth was outside BPS's marketplace category.
  - An arithmetic decomposition, not entry dynamics. Consistent with H2.
- **THESIS:**
  - **How the shares are computed (log shares):** ln(1.274)/ln(1.406) ≈ 0.71 and ln(1.1531)/ln(1.1708) ≈ 0.90. The pieces multiply exactly: 1.274 × 1.1036 ≈ 1.406.
  - **Alternative count:** BPS's alternative 2023 count changes the size, not the conclusion.
  - **Supporting figures:** 95.33% of e-commerce businesses used instant messaging. 28.63% of marketplace users keep complete financial statements against 12.25% of non-users; that is an association.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **The split:** total online selling = number of shops × selling per shop. So growth comes from two places: more shops, or each shop selling more.
  - **2022–23:** total +40.6%, made of shops +27.4% and per-shop +10.4%. About 7 of every 10 parts of the growth came from more shops.
  - **2023–24:** about 9 of 10 came from more shops; each shop sold almost the same (+1.5%).
  - **Where the growth was:** marketplace-app selling barely grew (+1.45%) while other channels, like chat and social media, grew +20.57%. Almost all the growth (98.46%) happened outside the app category, where app numbers can't see it, even if every app reports perfectly.
  - **What it's not:** this is splitting a total into pieces, not proof of *why* shops joined.
- **PICTURE:** City-wide online selling = number of vendors × sales per vendor.

  **2023–24:** vendors +15%, sales per vendor +1.5%, so about 9 of every 10 parts of the growth came from more vendors.

  **Where the growth happened:** selling inside marketplaces grew +1.45%, while WhatsApp and Instagram sellers grew +20.57%. So 98% of the growth happened outside the market gates, where neither the owner's book nor the stall receipts can see it, even if every market reports perfectly.
- **TRAP:** Say "participating businesses", not "new sellers entering"; the count doesn't show entry.

### 15 · Not Yet Reconcilable
- **SLIDE:**
  - **Levels:** the BPS marketplace category was about US$13.2B in 2023 (Rp200.68T), 18.2% of national e-commerce. Tokopedia V plus the Shopee estimate come to about US$37.9B; either alone is larger.
  - **Why not like-for-like:** BPS assigns sales to statistical channels; platforms use their own scope and definitions.
  - **Growth 2023→24:** e-commerce +17.08%; e-money shopping +30.47%; mobile banking +82.84%; QRIS +186.98%. Each system tells its own growth story; payments are context, not sales estimates.
  - The thesis reconciles definitions first.
- **THESIS:** Payment measures grew 1.8 to 11 times as fast as e-commerce value.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **The levels don't line up:** the government says all marketplace selling was about 13 billion dollars, yet one app alone reports more than that.
  - **That doesn't mean someone is wrong.** They sort things differently. The government puts each sale in a "channel" box; each app counts everything under its own scope and definitions. Which pieces differ is exactly what reconciliation has to find out.
  - **Payments aren't sales either:** payment apps grew even faster, but people pay bills and send money too.
  - **So nobody can subtract one number from another yet.** Making the definitions match comes first; that's thesis work.
- **PICTURE:** The city says all markets together sold 13; one market's own receipts already say 16 or more.

  It's like two people counting a crowd. One counts people inside the fence using the city's category rules. The other counts everyone who walked through his own gates, by his own rules.

  Before you compare the counts, you must agree on what "a market sale" means. The bank's QR count isn't sales either: it also includes paying bills and sending money to family.
- **TRAP:** Whatever the follow-up, the answer is that they don't measure the same thing yet, and the thesis reconciles that. Don't defend the gap.

## IV. WHAT IT MEANS

### 16 · Robustness and Boundaries
- **SLIDE:**
  - **Q&A:**
    - One outlier? No; without Blibli 2022–23 (+430.61 pp), p = 0.006.
    - Dependent years? One median per firm, p = 0.024.
    - Constructed figures? Grab and Shopee are excluded from H1.
    - Different business models? The benchmark spans very different models, yet shows 7.1 vs 38.9 pp; using growth also limits level comparability concerns.
    - Causal? No, it's a measurement and comparability study.
  - **Does not imply:** hidden GDP, unpaid tax, investor mispricing, or error in any indicator.
  - **Limits:** three firms, a purposive benchmark, two BPS transitions, levels not yet reconciled.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **What the study shows:** the numbers disagree in a way bigger than normal, and the result survives the obvious attacks.
  - **What it doesn't say:** that the country is secretly richer, that anyone dodged tax, that investors got fooled, or that any number is wrong.
  - **Its honest limits:** few firms, a hand-picked comparison group, and government surveys that changed method.
- **PICTURE:** The study is a thermometer, not a doctor. It shows the readings disagree more than normal, and that this holds after the obvious checks. It doesn't say why, and it doesn't say anyone cheated, dodged tax, or hid money.

### 17 · The Complete Chain
- **SLIDE:** Revenue → platform commerce (H1: inconsistent) → national online commerce (H2: consistent). Checked: within-platform divergence, benchmark test, national decomposition, channel split. Open: level reconciliation.
- **PLAIN:** Two steps from the app's income to the whole economy, and both leak. Income doesn't follow the app's selling, and the app's selling doesn't capture where national growth happens. One piece is still unfinished: making the totals comparable.
- **PICTURE:** Two leaky pipes in a row: owner's book → stall sales → city-wide selling.
  - **Leak 1:** the cut % changes.
  - **Leak 2:** growth happens outside the gates.

  Measuring at the first tap tells you little about the reservoir. One pipe joint, making the city's and the markets' totals comparable, isn't fitted yet.

### 18 · Contribution
- **SLIDE:**
  - **Three open questions answered so far:**
    - D is benchmarked and diverges far more in Indonesia.
    - Gaps are large and moving, and one major divergence reconciles to incentives.
    - National growth comes mostly from more businesses, outside the marketplace category.
  - **For finance:** revenue growth from more commerce and from keeping a bigger share look identical in revenue but needn't carry the same information.
  - **Framework:** match, size the gap, test stability, benchmark and diagnose.
- **THESIS:** Will complete:
  - quarterly pairs and a longer benchmark;
  - diagnosis of each large divergence;
  - reconciliation of platform and BPS definitions;
  - a market test of whether the stock market distinguishes the two kinds of revenue growth.
- **PLAIN:**
  - **For finance:** income can grow because more is sold, or because the app keeps a bigger slice. On paper both look like "income up", but they mean different things. The first means the business grew; the second means it squeezed more from the same or less.
  - **The four-step check before using one number for another:** (1) make sure both cover the same period and business; (2) measure the gap; (3) check whether the gap stays stable over time; (4) compare with similar apps and explain big jumps.
  - **Later:** more data points, explaining each jump, making the government and app numbers comparable, and testing whether stock investors notice the difference.
