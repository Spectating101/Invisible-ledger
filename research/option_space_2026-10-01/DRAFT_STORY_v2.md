# The Invisible Ledger: the story, small scale (version 2, 12 Oct 2026)

The whole story at reading level, before any chapter is written. Same order as `NARRATIVE_ARC.md`, same claims as `DRAFT_STORY_v1.md` (with the 10 Oct repairs), same voice as `GOLDEN_PITCH.md`.

Every unit has the same five beats: **the question** in everyday words; **what you would expect**; **what we see**, with one picture and at most two numbers; **why**; **what it means**, ending on the next question. Then a short **How sure are we** box holds the tests, checks and failures, so the story stays clean and the rigour stays in one predictable place.

---

## A. Who reads the online economy, and with what?

Nobody watches the online economy directly. Everyone reads it through a few numbers.

You might expect those numbers to agree, since they describe the same buying and selling. They are built for different jobs. Investors read what platforms report: how much passed through them and how much they kept. Statistics offices read surveys of the people selling. Tax offices are starting to read the records platforms keep about their sellers.

That last use is spreading. The OECD wrote model rules for it in 2020. The European Union has required it since 2023. The Philippines began taxing marketplace sellers through the platforms in 2024, and Viet Nam in 2025.

Each of these numbers stands in for something nobody sees whole. A stand-in works while it moves with the thing it stands for. When it stops, two honest numbers can tell opposite stories about the same year.

So the question is simple. When does a stand-in stop working, and how much does it leave out?

*Sources: OECD (2020; goods module 2021); Directive (EU) 2021/514; BIR RR 16-2023; Decree 117/2025/ND-CP (`tables/src/platform_seller_rules_intl.csv`).*

## A-B. Why look at Indonesia?

To test a stand-in, you need a place where it is likely to break, and a way to look underneath it.

Indonesia offers both. Its platforms keep a very thin slice of each sale. Most of its online sellers never use a marketplace; they sell through WhatsApp, Instagram and other chat and social apps. And in 2023 it had a loud public argument about online selling. Market traders in Jakarta's Tanah Abang said cheap goods on TikTok Shop were taking their customers. The government banned shopping inside social media apps, and TikTok Shop closed in early October.

Indonesia also lets us look underneath. Its platforms report both what passes through them and what they keep. BPS, the statistics office, asks sellers how their year went and when they started selling online. I bought those survey answers for three years.

The research question is the proposal's: how much activity stays invisible when platforms are measured by their revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth? Two hypotheses test the two numbers people lean on most. H1 asks whether a platform's revenue grows in step with its sales. H2 asks whether national online growth comes mainly from more businesses selling online.

*Sources: AFP (25 Sep 2023); ANTARA (28 Sep 2023); Permendag 31/2023; proposal sections 1.1 and 3.2.*

## 1. How much does revenue leave out?

**The question.** If you only see a platform's revenue, how much of the buying do you see?

**What you would expect.** Revenue sounds like the platform's size. Investors value platforms largely on it.

**What we see.** Of every 100 rupiah buyers spend through Indonesia's large platforms, revenue shows about 7. In 2023, on Tokopedia, Shopee and Grab, the rest came to about US$40 billion. This gap is the invisible wedge: transaction value minus revenue.

**Why.** A platform is a go-between. Most of what passes through it is the sellers' sale, not the platform's income. Revenue is only the part the platform keeps.

**What it means.** Revenue is a small slice of the buying. The platforms themselves carry only about a fifth of online sales; the rest moves through chat, social media and sellers' own sites. A small slice can still be a fair guide, though, if it stays the same size. So the next question is whether it does.

> **How sure are we.** Transaction value US$43.23bn, revenue US$3.16bn, wedge US$40.07bn (2023). Shopee's assumed take rate of 9-11% moves the wedge only between US$39.86bn and US$40.29bn; dropping any one platform leaves at least US$20.70bn. The platforms' share of online sales is 16-26% in BPS's survey (2022 microdata; 2023 and 2024 published). The wedge stays at 97-99% of transaction value every year for the three platforms that report both numbers.

---

## 2. Does revenue follow sales? (H1)

**The question.** If people buy 10 percent more through a platform, does its revenue rise about 10 percent too?

**What you would expect.** Yes. Abroad, that is mostly what happens. H1 says the same holds in Indonesia.

**What we see.** It does not. From year to year, the share Indonesian platforms keep from each sale moved about three times as much as at platforms abroad (Figure: the thin slice). Their revenue and their sales went separate ways. H1 is rejected.

**Why.** Indonesian platforms did not change their prices more than platforms abroad. Both raised or cut what they keep by about one rupiah per 100 sold, in a typical year. The difference is the slice. A platform abroad keeps about 17 rupiah per 100, so one more rupiah is a small raise. An Indonesian marketplace keeps under 2, so the same rupiah is a raise of a half. This is the invisible wedge at work: the larger it is next to revenue, the more a small price change shakes revenue growth.

**What it means.** In Indonesia, revenue is a fragile guide to sales. A small price decision can look like a boom or a slump in sales. So what were those price decisions?

> **How sure are we.** 5 Indonesian and regional platforms against 27 abroad, one result per firm: p = 0.004. Dropping any one Indonesian platform: worst p = 0.016. Growth divergence: 27.0 vs 6.5 points, p = 0.005. GoTo's two segments as one company: p = 0.003. The main sample alone (8 comparisons) gives p = 0.06 on a different firm-level test. The quarterly version does not hold (p = 0.19); those data mostly cover the calmer years after 2023. The "same price change, thinner slice" comparison was made after the results and is exploratory (in points p = 0.65, relative p = 0.003; 26 foreign firms, the same with 27). Exact link: growth divergence D = (1 + gV)(m1 - m0)/m0.

---

## 3. What were the price changes?

**The question.** If revenue moved with prices, what did the platforms actually change?

**What you would expect.** Higher fees, perhaps. That is what "charging more" usually means.

**What we see.** Mostly, the platforms stopped paying for so many discounts. Tokopedia is the clearest case. In 2023, the value of what people bought on it fell 9 percent. Its revenue rose 53 percent (Figure: Tokopedia 2023).

**Why.** In Tokopedia's accounts, the discounts it pays for are deducted from its revenue. When Tokopedia cut its discounts, from Rp4.1 trillion to Rp2.8 trillion, its revenue rose without anyone buying more. That cut explains about 60 percent of its revenue rise. The rest came from higher fees.

**What it means.** In 2022-24, Indonesian platform revenue mostly reported pricing decisions, not more buying. That raises a question about the people who read revenue most closely. Did investors follow revenue, or sales?

> **How sure are we.** Tokopedia: lower incentives explain 60.6% of the net revenue rise (GoTo annual report 2023, Note 29). Across Indonesian years, how much a platform keeps was about two thirds of the movement in revenue (66.5%), against about a quarter abroad (26.7%); this compares the sizes of the two parts before they offset, not shares of net growth. Grab 2022-23: repricing is 3.5 of a 3.9-point rise. GoTo rides and delivery 2023-25: fewer discounts explain 67% of a 114% rise. Blibli 2022-23: fewer discounts explain 63%; part of the rest is a shift toward travel.

## 4. Did investors follow revenue or sales?

**The question.** If revenue mostly reported price decisions, were investors misled?

**What you would expect.** Investors value platforms largely on revenue, so you might expect market value to follow it.

**What we see.** Across 28 listed platforms, market value moved with sales. I registered this test before collecting the market data.

**Why.** Earlier studies of internet firms found the same habit: markets price signs of activity, such as visitors and traffic, beyond reported income.

**What it means.** Market value followed the buying underneath revenue. The public argument of 2023, though, was about growth. So was 2023 really a boom?

> **How sure are we.** 28 firms, 138 firm-years: market value moved with growth in transaction value (b = 0.60, p = 0.002); an association, not a measure of how investors decide. The second registered prediction, that revenue growth from a higher take rate is valued less, did not hold (b = 0.33, p = 0.20); it holds only when segment series are added. Market-value change includes new share issues, so this is not a stock-return result. Related work: Trueman, Wong and Zhang (2000); Rajgopal, Venkatachalam and Kotha (2003).

---

## 5. Was 2023 a boom?

**The question.** The headline figures said online selling boomed in 2023. Did it?

**What you would expect.** Yes. BPS later reported online sales up 41 percent and online sellers up 27 percent.

**What we see.** Buying through the platforms did not boom. Five of six Indonesian measures of buying through platforms grew more slowly than ordinary household spending. Three of them fell (Figure: 2023).

**Why.** Platform revenue rose because of pricing, as units 2 and 3 showed. The buying underneath it stood still or shrank.

**What it means.** Inside the apps, 2023 was not a boom. Yet the official figures were up strongly. So where did their growth come from?

> **How sure are we.** Household spending +9.4% (nominal). Tokopedia transaction value -8.9%, GoTo rides and delivery -10.5%, Bank Indonesia e-commerce -4.7%, Momentum Works +3.5%, Bukalapak +6.9%; the exception is Blibli, +34.7%. BPS: e-commerce value +40.6%, online sellers +27.4% (about 819,000). Each measure in its own unit.

---

## 6. Did the boom move off the apps?

**The question.** If the apps did not boom, did online selling grow somewhere the apps cannot see?

**What you would expect.** Probably. Most Indonesian online sellers never use a marketplace. They sell through chat and social media. If growth was out of the apps' sight, it would be there.

**What we see.** The sellers do not describe a boom. BPS asks each business how its online sales changed. Among sellers already in business, most said their sales were flat or down. Their most common complaint was a lack of customers.

**Why.** Sellers reported weak demand. More sellers named it as their main problem in 2023 than the year before.

**What it means.** The boom is not in what sellers themselves reported. Yet the official count of sellers rose by about 819,000. So who were the extra sellers?

> **How sure are we.** Existing sellers in 2023: 24% up, 44% the same, 32% down; average reported change -4.3%. Main complaint "lack of demand": 41%, up from 35% in 2022. Where BPS and the platforms look at the same place, the marketplaces, they agree: BPS's own marketplace sales grew 0.2% in 2023 in the middle scenario (at most 13%). About 96% of sellers use chat or social media.

---

## 7. Where did the extra sellers come from? (H2)

**The question.** If 819,000 more people were selling online, were they new businesses?

**What you would expect.** H2 says national growth comes mainly from more businesses. On BPS's published numbers, it does: H2 holds. The proposal read this as new sellers appearing in chat and social media. It also named a limit: the published numbers cannot tell a new seller from one counted for the first time.

**What we see.** The survey answers can tell them apart. Every seller says the year they started selling online. Only about 513,000 started in 2023. About half of the rise were sellers who say they were selling before 2023 (Figure: sellers' own answers).

**Why.** If those sellers were already selling, the count grew because it reached more of them, or because some came back after a break. The extra count is concentrated where the survey itself grew. The next year looks normal again. Both fit a survey that reached further in 2023.

**What it means.** H2 holds on paper. But about half of the official jump in sellers was not first-time entry. So who were these sellers?

> **How sure are we.** The entry test was written down on 2 October, before the survey files were opened: at least about 1 in 5 sellers (21.5%) should say they started in 2023; the answer was about 1 in 7 (13.45%). The part not explained by first-time entry is 37-56% of the rise, depending on how many sellers stopped. In a sampling-error check using BPS's later published error rates, the gap is 4.6 standard errors (2.3 at twice the error). Count growth follows growth in survey size across provinces, not the share of new sellers. The cause, wider reach or sellers returning, is not separated by these data; returning sellers stay the named alternative (units 8-9). If recall errors run forward, as in Neter and Waksberg (1964), the entry shortfall is conservative.

---

## 8. Who were the extra sellers?

**The question.** If they were not first-time sellers, what kind of businesses were they?

**What you would expect.** Online growth stories usually feature young people starting something new.

**What we see.** The extra count leaned the other way. It was concentrated among long-running businesses, many opened before 2010, with older owners. More of them were outside Java, and many sold food or made things (Figure: who they were).

**Why.** These are businesses that went online quietly, through chat and social media, often years after they opened. They are easy for any count to miss.

**What it means.** The extra count looks like established small businesses, not start-ups. These are the people the original question was about: real business that numbers easily miss. Could they simply be sellers coming back after a break?

> **How sure are we.** Exploratory. The surveys do not follow the same businesses across years, so this compares groups: each group's 2023 count against its 2022 count, net of that group's normal yearly change in 2020-22. Business opened before 2010: 41% of the extra count vs 18% of the 2022 count. Owner aged 50 or over: 41% vs 29%. Outside Java: 36% vs 24%. Food and drink or making goods: 53% vs 39%. The raw lean toward men disappears under this control. Education codes for one round are inferred.

---

## 9. Could they be sellers back from a break?

**The question.** Some people stop selling for a while and come back. Could they explain the jump?

**What you would expect.** Possibly. Small businesses open and close all the time.

**What we see.** Someone back from a break usually sells for only part of the year. Yet part-year selling fell in 2023, even within the same groups of sellers (Figure: part-year selling).

**Why.** Small firms that close and restart tend to be young, with young owners. The extra count leaned toward the opposite: old businesses, older owners.

**What it means.** Returning sellers are unlikely to explain most of the jump, but the data cannot rule them out. Either way, the official jump was not mainly new businesses. Then why did two state offices tell opposite stories?

> **How sure are we.** Among sellers who started by 2021, part-year selling fell from 472,000 to 318,000; it falls for every start-year group (2020, 2021, 2022). This is a diagnostic: without linked histories, returns can be offset by exits or by sellers moving to full-year selling, and January returns are possible. The month question changed format between rounds. On who restarts: McKenzie and Paffhausen (2019). Returning happens every year; to explain 2023 it would have to spike in that year alone.

---

## 10. Why did two state offices disagree?

**The question.** In 2023, Bank Indonesia's figure for online shopping fell about 5 percent. BPS's rose 41 percent. Is one of them wrong?

**What you would expect.** That one must be wrong.

**What we see.** Where both look at the same place, the marketplaces, they agree that growth was slow. They part ways only outside the marketplaces.

**Why.** The two offices measure different parts of online selling. Bank Indonesia's figure comes from large platforms. BPS's survey also reaches sellers on chat and social media. That is where BPS's count rose by more than first-time entry can explain.

**What it means.** The disagreement is not a contest between a right and a wrong number. Each number sees a different slice. That matters now, because Indonesia has started writing rules on these slices.

> **How sure are we.** Bank Indonesia -4.7%, BPS +40.6% (2023). BPS's marketplace slice of 2022 is 0.16-0.68 times Bank Indonesia's figure, so the gap is not in the marketplaces. Bank Indonesia's method is not public. Sizes cannot be fully reconciled; the reasons are named in the appendix (definitions, coverage at the top, counts, mix).

## 11. Who do the 2026 rules reach?

**The question.** Indonesia's new rules lean on these numbers. Who do they actually reach?

**What you would expect.** A tax on online sellers should reach online sellers.

**What we see.** The new marketplace tax can collect from about 1 online seller in 20. It does put every marketplace seller on record. But marketplace sellers are fewer than 1 in 5 of all online sellers (Figure: tax reach).

**Why.** The tax is built on platform records, so it reaches only sellers on platforms. The other rules work the same way. The ride-hailing commission cap covers motorbike rides only. The discount rule targets discounts the platforms were already cutting.

**What it means.** Each rule reaches only what its number sees. The long-running chat sellers of unit 8 sit outside the marketplace rules. The 2026 economic census will reach further into that same group. When its results feed the official count, the next jump in sellers may again be counting rather than growth.

> **How sure are we.** Reach, not effects: no rule's effect is measured. Can be taxed: marketplace sellers with Rp300m+ a year, an upper bound (the exemption is Rp500m), about 5% of sellers and 17-24% of online sales value. On record: 17.8% of sellers (2023). Only 13.5% of sellers outside the marketplaces want to join one. Rule texts: PMK 37/2025, Permendag 19/2026; Perpres 27/2026 as announced. Scheduled checks: 27 Oct (GoTo), 1 Nov (tax start), early and mid Nov.

---

## 12. So what was 2023?

**The question.** Back to the start: was 2023 the boom the headline figures described?

**What we see.** The headline figures that described a boom were measuring something narrower. Platform revenue followed prices: the platforms kept more of each sale. About half of the rise in the official seller count was not first-time entry. The tax office's coming records cover a minority of sellers.

**What it means.** The sellers who said it was a hard year were describing what the evidence shows. Less had moved online in 2023 than the headlines suggested.

---

## B-C. What carries beyond Indonesia?

Some of this belongs to Indonesia. The thin slice does: most platforms abroad keep far more of each sale. The survey finding does too: it is one country, one survey, one year.

What carries is the check. A platform's revenue follows its sales only while it keeps the same share of each sale, and its own reports show whether it does. A count of online sellers follows new businesses only while first-time entry explains its growth, and one question about the year a seller started tests that. A rule built on platform records reaches only the sellers on the platforms. Several countries are now building such rules.

---

## C. What should each user of these numbers do?

**Investors:** ask whether a platform's growth came from more buying or from keeping more of each sale. Market value already seems to follow buying; anyone reading revenue as growth should too.

**Statistics offices:** when a count of online sellers jumps, ask whether the sellers are new. Where selling runs through chat apps, a count can rise without new businesses. In Indonesia, the extra count leaned toward older owners of long-running small businesses, away from the big cities.

**Tax offices:** before building on platform records, find out how many sellers work outside the platforms. In Indonesia, it is most of them.

**The closing line.** The numbers we use to see the digital economy are stand-ins. They hold while prices and counting stay the same. In Indonesia in 2023, both shifted at once. The headline figures described a boom; the people selling described a hard year.
