# Cold read of the thesis draft, 15 October 2026

Reader: an examiner for a finance master's who had not seen the project before. I read Chapters 1 to 5 (`papers/thesis_2026-10/ch1`–`ch5`), then `drafts/ch6_draft_2026-10-14.md` and `drafts/ch7_draft_2026-10-14.md`, in that order. I read no planning file first. I looked at the figures the chapters link to. I checked no number against a source. Where I say a number is wrong, I mean it is inconsistent with the thesis's own definitions or with another chapter.

**Overall.** The draft is far easier to read than most theses I examine. Most sentences are short, active and point-first, and the voice holds steady from Chapter 1 to Chapter 7. The trouble spots are local. They are (a) a few sentences where the logic is packed into one clause, (b) terms and survey-year names that shift, (c) three or four places where two chapters contradict each other, and (d) Chapter 4 and Section 5.9, where the method detail gets dense.

---

## 1. Chapter by chapter

### Chapter 1. Introduction

**Keep.** The opening is good. The Tanah Abang scene, then two official figures moving in opposite directions, then Tokopedia's revenue rising while its sales fell: three short scenes make the problem concrete before any term appears. "The two official figures moved in opposite directions" is a strong one-line point.

**Where I got lost or had to reread**
- Opening, para 5: "If part of that growth is a platform keeping more of each sale, then part of the price is a pricing decision." I read "price" as the platform's price, not its share price, and the sentence went in a circle. I needed a second pass.
- Opening, para 4: "about 60 percent of the extra revenue came from smaller discounts and incentives, which a platform pays for out of what it earns." Sixty percent of what, and on which measure? The clause after the comma made me wonder whether incentives are a cost or a deduction from revenue. Chapter 3 later says they are a deduction.
- §1.1, research question: "how much economic activity remains invisible when digital platforms are measured through their reported revenue, and when does that choice change the conclusion about growth?" At about 30 words it is long. It is also inherited, so it may have to stay. The two-halves paragraph after it rescues it.

**Paragraphs that did not follow**
- §1.1, para 1 is a change of voice ("This thesis sets out to change how the digital economy is measured … Decisions worth trillions of dollars rest on numbers … who is in it"). Everything before it is careful and evidence-led. This paragraph is advocacy, and it promises more than the thesis delivers. The thesis does not measure "who is in it", apart from the profile in §5.8, and it does not show "millions of the people who sell online are outside those figures". The word "missing" also invites the reading of the wedge as missing activity, which §3.2 and §7.4 reject.
- Opening, para 5 jumps from one company (Tokopedia) to US$27 trillion of world online sales and then to investors. The bridge sentence ("Platform and online sales figures carry weight well beyond Indonesia") works. The investor sentence that follows is the weak link (see above).

**Terms used before I knew them.** In the objectives: "monetization", "revenue recognition", "reporting scope", "payment figures". "Entry test" appears in §1.1 with only a gloss. That is fine for an introduction, but "monetization" never comes back in plain words.

**Numbers.** The levy figures, "about US$60 million a year on one measure of the market and about US$300 million on another", do not name the two measures. I found them only in §6.3.

**Inconsistency with later chapters**
- §1.1 says "A further analysis estimates how many sellers a rule … can reach". §1.3 says Chapter 3 sets out "four further analyses". I could not tell which was right until Chapter 3.
- Chapter 1 gives Tokopedia's story as "about 60 percent of the extra revenue came from smaller discounts and incentives". Table 5.4 says the opposite on the thesis's main measure: higher fees 76, lower incentives 45. Section 5.3 explains that there are two views. But the introduction picks the view that Chapter 5 does not use as its main one, so it previews a result that Table 5.4 does not show.
- "In Indonesia, platform revenue also grew faster than the commerce behind it": the H1 group includes Grab's region-wide on-demand business. The scope is wider than "Indonesia".

**What the chapter did, in one sentence:** In 2023 Indonesia's revenue figures and national figures told different growth stories. The thesis asks how much revenue leaves out and when that changes the growth conclusion, and it previews the answer: revenue is a thin and unsteady share, and about half of the 2023 rise in sellers was not new entry. *I could state this easily.*

### Chapter 2. Literature Review

**Keep.** This is the cleanest chapter. Each section opens with its point, gives the evidence and ends on what stays open. Table 2.1 is explained in the text, and the three numbered questions in §2.4 connect clearly to what follows. The IFRS 15 example ("a rider pays 100 … such a company records 20") is exactly the right size.

**Where I had to reread**
- §2.2, para 2: "National accounts … count only the fee as the platform's output". "Output" is never defined, and it matters later because the wedge "does not measure missing output" (§3.2, §7.4).
- §2.2, para 2 moves from national accounts to night-time lights to export margins in four sentences. Each idea is fine, but the paragraph has three claims, and I lost the thread on what "in the same spirit" refers to.

**Paragraphs that did not follow**
- §2.2, para 4: "Small firms that stop are more often young firms with young owners (McKenzie and Paffhausen 2019)." Here I could not see why it mattered. Its purpose appears only in §3.5 and §5.8. One clause on why it matters (returning sellers) would fix this.

**As a finance examiner.** The finance literature here is thin. It consists of Bowen et al. 2002, Trueman et al. 2000/2001, Rajgopal et al. 2003 and Givoly et al. 2019. These cover only about one paragraph. Nothing covers the disclosure of non-GAAP key performance indicators by platforms, gross-versus-net revenue reporting after IFRS 15/ASC 606, or how analysts use GMV in valuing e-commerce firms. Yet the thesis claims a contribution "for investors" (§1.2, §7.2). Prof. Kong's "cite finance work" comment would apply here.

**What the chapter did, in one sentence:** Platform economics, accounting, official statistics and tax research each explain why platform and national figures can differ, but none has measured how far they differ over time in one country, and none has done so for Indonesia. *Easy to state.*

### Chapter 3. Framework and Hypotheses

**Keep.** Table 3.1 as the single home for definitions is excellent. The 21.5 percent threshold in §3.5 is derived in plain arithmetic that a junior-high reader could follow ("for every 100 sellers a year earlier there were 127.4"). The recall-error paragraph is a model of turning a caveat into an argument. The worked Tokopedia example in §3.2 is the right idea.

**Where I got lost or had to reread**
- Opening, para 3: "The three objectives set in Chapter 1 sit inside this map. Measuring the wedge and tracing its large moves serve the first question, and comparing platform figures with national and payment figures serves the second. The third question is answered by a further analysis." Objectives 1 to 3 and questions 1 to 3 do not line up. Objective 3 serves question 2, and question 3 has no objective. I reread this three times.
- Opening note under Figure 3.1: "The platform circle differs from the marketplace channel in the survey". I wanted to know how it differs. The answer (BPS's "marketplace" includes Gojek and Grab sellers) appears only in §4.8 and §5.9.
- §3.2: "D was 0.91 × 0.68 = 0.62". The 0.68 is not derived in the text. I had to work out (2.5 − 1.5)/1.5 myself, which gives 0.67, so the rounding left me unsure.
- §3.2: "Equation (4) shows that D is the change in the take rate divided by its starting level." Equation (4) also has the factor (1 + g(V)), so the sentence is not quite what the equation says.
- §3.2: "The ecosystem ratio also governs how far a change in the take rate moves revenue growth." The next paragraph's examples work, but the link from E to the result is never shown (E + 1 = 1/m). A reader is left to ask what E adds beyond the take rate itself.
- §3.3: "Measured in logarithms, the growth of revenue splits exactly …". The log explanation that follows is good. But this is the point where a non-finance reader would stop, and the plain-words version ("growth in revenue = growth in sales + growth in the share kept") could come first.

**Paragraphs that did not follow**
- §3.4, after Table 3.2: "The share of transaction value that a platform keeps is not fixed … (Rochet and Tirole 2003) … (Evans and Schmalensee 2016)." This and the next paragraph repeat §2.1 almost line for line. Having just read the hypothesis table, I was taken back to theory.
- §3.6, the market-value paragraph moves from Trueman et al. to "the two sources of growth need not be worth the same" to the equation. It holds, but just barely.

**Tables and numbers.** Table 3.2's H1 row says the test is "Changes in Indonesia far larger than abroad". "Far larger" is never given a threshold. I learn in Chapter 4 that it is a one-sided rank test, but the framework does not say what result would count.

**One term per thing.** Table 3.1 itself admits that an online seller is "also called … a seller or a business". The national figure appears as "national e-commerce value", "online sales value", "online commerce" and "online selling". In the end I assumed these were one thing, but I was not sure until Chapter 5.

**Section endings.** Most sections end on a bridge to the next chapter ("Chapter 4 describes …"), not on what stays open.

**What the chapter did, in one sentence:** It defines transaction value, revenue, the take rate, the wedge, the ecosystem ratio and growth divergence, and it shows that revenue growth splits into volume, fees and incentives. It then sets H1 (take rates move more in Indonesia than abroad), H2 (more sellers drive most national growth), a 21.5 percent entry threshold and four further analyses. *I could state it, but only as a list. The chapter has more parts than a reader can hold.*

### Chapter 4. Data and Methods

**Keep.** The opening four paragraphs explain why Indonesia makes the tests possible, and that is persuasive. Table 4.1, with scope stated per series, is exactly what Prof. Kong asked for. Table 4.2's check that the weights reproduce the BPS count is reassuring. "The test was written down and dated in the project's code archive, called the repository, before the files were opened" is a good model of defining a term at first use.

**Where I got lost or had to reread**
- §4.1, para 5: "applied in two ways. For the 19 platforms added, the thesis drops any year in which own goods exceed half of revenue. For the eight chosen first, it keeps a pair only when the company's reporting scope did not change". The second rule is not about own goods, so I did not see how it applies the third rule. I also never learned why there were "eight chosen first" and "19 added".
- §4.2, last sentence: "The thesis drops each platform in turn, and it repeats the exercise for every platform-year between 2020 and 2025 that the filings allow." Dropping a platform from a sum of three, and repeating a 2023 sum for every year, are two different things. I could not picture either.
- §4.3: "They repeat the test with growth divergence in points as Chapter 3 defines it." What does "in points" mean? Chapter 3 defines D as a share.
- §4.4: "Seven windows are traced". "Window" is never defined. "The same split is made for the wedge" leaves me to guess how.
- §4.6: "BPS prints two counts of businesses for 2023". Why two? This comes back in §4.7 (23.9 percent) and §5.6 (90 or 71 percent) and is never explained.
- §4.6: "reproduce two published shares of sellers who keep complete financial statements, 28.63 and 12.25 percent". Two shares of what? I found out only in §5.9 that they are marketplace and other sellers.
- §4.7: "If the weights had matched BPS's other count for 2023, the threshold would have been 23.9 percent, but they did not." This is a counterfactual about an unexplained second count, with "they" referring back to the weights. I reread it three times.
- §4.7, para 4 is a five-check list compressed into five sentences. "They profile who the extra sellers are, with each group's count in 2023 compared with what its own yearly change in 2020 to 2022 would give" was the hardest.
- §4.8: "The 2023 questionnaire asks for monthly revenue but the bracket limits are annual size classes, so both readings are computed and the one that passes is kept." As an examiner I read this as choosing a reading by its result (see question 3 below). As a reader I also tripped on the next sentence: "If the lower brackets alone already exceed the total, value shares are reported as ranges."
- §4.9: "Beyond the rules of Section 3.6". Section 3.6 has no rules, and the comparison rules are in §3.1. "Six measures of buying through platforms" are never listed in the text.

**Terms used before defined**
- "Registered" first appears in §4.7 ("which was registered") and is defined only in §4.10.
- "Returners" appears in §4.7 without a gloss.

**Survey-year names.** §4.6 sets a rule: name a file by its round and a year by its activity year. The text then uses "the 2023 file", "the 2024 file", "the 2023 questionnaire", "the survey for 2023" and "the 2021 and 2023 files". I had to keep Table 4.2 open from here to the end. In §7.4, "The survey for 2023 has no revenue amounts" looks as if it contradicts §4.8's "The 2023 questionnaire asks for monthly revenue". It does not, but only because of the naming rule.

**What is buried.** §4.10 says the main H1 test is exploratory: "The test with the larger groups in Section 4.3 belongs here, because it was set up after a first test on the three marketplaces." For the headline hypothesis, that belongs in §4.3, not in a section about labels.

**Market-value sample (§4.5).** It "adds Airbnb, Booking, DoorDash, Lyft and Uber, and it lacks Despegar, Mercado Libre, Ozon, Rakuten and Sea." I wanted one sentence on why. Why are Uber and Booking out of the H1 group, and why is Sea out of the market sample? The Indonesian side of the market sample is Bukalapak alone.

**What the chapter did, in one sentence:** It sets five Indonesian and regional platform series (15 yearly pairs) against 27 foreign platforms (158 pairs), and three BPS survey files that reproduce the published counts. It then describes each test and labels each as registered, exploratory or descriptive. *I could state it, but I would not trust myself to explain the value-by-bracket method afterwards.*

### Chapter 5. Results

**Keep.** The chapter opening gives both links in four sentences, and that is the best summary in the thesis. Table 5.2 with its labels column is excellent. The cohort argument in §5.7 ("A cohort should only shrink between surveys … In the survey for 2023 they rose by 6 percent") is the most convincing evidence in the thesis and is told plainly. The part-year argument against returners in §5.8 is clear and clever. §5.3's "The windows of one platform overlap, so they are not seven independent cases" is the kind of honesty that disarms an examiner.

**Where I got lost or had to reread**
- §5.1: "The ecosystem ratio was about 13, so almost 13 dollars of commerce passed through the platforms for every dollar of revenue." By the thesis's own definition, E is the *wedge* per dollar of revenue. Commerce per dollar of revenue is E + 1, about 14. A careful reader with Table 3.1 open will catch this.
- §5.1: "Shopee's ratio of 9 follows from the assumed take rate, and Grab's ratio of about 8 is its company-wide ratio. Neither is separate evidence for Indonesia." "Neither" came after three platforms had just been named, so I paused.
- §5.2: "Revenue grew faster than transaction value in 13 of the 15 … pairs. In two of them, revenue and transaction value moved in opposite directions." Is that two of the 13, or the other two?
- §5.2: "In that sample, revenue growth and transaction value growth had a correlation of 0.10 in Indonesia and 0.98 abroad, excluding Sea." Why exclude Sea? It is not explained.
- §5.2: "Figure 5.1 shows the three measures side by side." "The three measures" already means W, E and D (§3.2). Here it means three other things.
- §5.3: "That count leaves the fall in volume inside both lines." It is opaque, and it is the hinge of the two-views argument.
- §5.4, last para: "Revenue, transaction value and market value all come from the platforms' own reports." Market value comes from LSEG prices (§4.5), not from the platforms. The sentence is also a weak bridge to §5.5.
- §5.7: "It had only a weak link to growth in the sample, at 0.35." "Growth in the sample" means growth in the number of survey rows, I think. "Rank correlation" is used one sentence before it is defined.
- §5.7: "Entrants account for 25 to 30 percent of that rise, and sellers not explained by entry for 15 to 17 percent. Existing sellers reported a mean change of about −4 percent, so the remaining 53 to 59 percent cannot be traced to them." I could not reconstruct this. - §5.9: "The annual reading of the brackets passes the validation rule, at 1.95 times BPS's total, while the monthly reading fails at 23 times. The lower brackets alone exceed BPS's total, so the shares are ranges." I could not follow this. As an examiner I also note that 1.95 sits just under the limit of 2.
- §5.10: "BPS's own marketplace slice grew by about 0.2 percent in the middle scenario. Across the scenarios it grew by 0.2 to 13 percent." Is the middle scenario also the lowest? I reread it.

**Tables and numbers not explained in the text**
- Table 5.1: "13.45 against 21.5 percent" is unrounded, while the text says "about 13". The label column mixes "Registered and descriptive" in one cell.
- Table 5.3: the quarterly row (0.071 against 0.049) and the "5 platforms against 26" row are not discussed. The 26 is explained only in the Figure 5.1 caption.
- Table 5.4: revenue growth of +465 and +679 percent at Blibli is never discussed. A reader wants one sentence on why revenue grew sevenfold. The note "Bukalapak's reports do not allow the parts to be separated" is attached to a table in which Bukalapak does not appear.

- §5.9: the World Bank prediction ("mean sales would stay below about US$6,600") is never motivated. I did not know why it mattered.

On the national side, H2 holds on the published figures, but about half the 2023 rise in the seller count was not new entry, and most online selling and its growth sit outside marketplaces. *I could state it, but only in two sentences, one per link. ## 3. Does the story hold from Chapter 1 to Chapter 7?

**Mostly yes.** The spine runs straight through. Two official figures disagree in 2023, revenue is a thin and moving share, the seller count jumped for reasons other than entry, the rules reach only the marketplace slice, and each figure is a fair guide only while its condition holds. Chapter 7's last paragraph closes the loop on Chapter 1's BPS–Bank Indonesia and Tokopedia scenes.

**Where it breaks or sags**
1. **TikTok and Tokopedia.** Chapter 1 opens with TikTok Shop being banned and closing. The thesis never says what happened next (TikTok's tie-up with Tokopedia). That event explains why Tokopedia's own series ends after 2023, and it resurfaces without warning in §6.2 ("TikTok Shop with Tokopedia"). The Tanah Abang scene is never returned to.
2. **Tokopedia's lever.** Chapter 1 says incentives drove Tokopedia's revenue (60 percent). Table 5.4 says fees did (76 against 45). §5.3 reconciles the two views, but the introduction previews the minority view.
3. **"Thin take rate" and the on-demand platforms.** Chapter 5 explains H1's rejection by thin take rates, but two of the five platforms are on-demand businesses with take rates near 8 to 11 percent (Figure 5.1), or about 22 percent in one quarter (§6.2). The story never says whether the thin-slice explanation covers them.
4. **The ambition paragraph.** §1.1 para 1 promises to show "who is in it" and "millions" outside the figures. Chapter 7 does not come back to it, and the thesis does not deliver it at that scale.
5. **A finding that gets lost.** §5.7's result that most of BPS's 2023 *value* rise cannot be traced to sellers' own answers is stronger than anything in Chapter 7's summary of the outer link. Chapter 7 does not mention it.

**Do Chapters 6 and 7 sound like 1 to 5?** Yes. The sentence length, point-first openings and plain words match. I could not have told that they were drafted separately. The two differences are that Chapter 6 relies more on news sources and legal citations, and that Chapter 7's limits section introduces new facts (19 sample rows, Grab revisions, education codes). New facts should not first appear in a conclusion.

## 4. The three hardest questions I would ask at the defence

**Q1. Is the rejection of H1 an Indonesian finding, or arithmetic about thin take rates?** Your own check shows that both groups changed take rates by about 1 point a year (p = 0.65), and the threefold gap in log changes comes from the starting level (1.6 against 17 percent). Any low-take-rate marketplace anywhere would then "reject H1". On top of that:
- The comparison group is mostly high-take-rate travel, freelance and delivery firms.
- The Indonesian group has five series, one of which (Tokopedia) contributes a single pair, and one (Grab) is region-wide.
- The main test was chosen after a narrower test.
- The quarterly test does not reject.

What is left of H1 that a finance reader should act on?
- *Does the text answer it?* **Partly.** §5.2 (para 5–6) and §7.1 (para 3) concede the base effect openly. §7.2 turns it into the investor lesson ("The thinner the take rate, the more a small change … moves revenue growth"). §7.3 limits the rejection to 2021–2024, and §7.4 gives the sample size. **Not answered:** whether the result holds against foreign platforms with similar thin take rates, whether it holds for the two on-demand series (which are not thin), and why H1 should be read as "rejected" rather than "explained by base level". The defence needs a ready one-sentence answer: H1 is about proportional growth, and thin take rates are *why* proportionality fails. That answer is only implicit now.

**Q2. What does the market-value analysis tell an investor about Indonesian platforms?** The sample is 28 firms and 138 firm-years, of which one firm (Bukalapak) is Indonesian. GoTo, Grab and Sea are absent. The dependent variable mixes price changes with share issuance. There are no controls for profitability, rates or dilution beyond year effects. The key prediction (β_V > β_m) fails in the main sample. The thesis's investor contribution (§1.2: "part of the price is a pricing decision") therefore has no market evidence behind it.
- *Does the text answer it?* **Partly.** §5.4 and §7.3 report the failure honestly. §7.4 calls the result an association, not a stock return. §7.5 names an event study as the next step. **Not answered:** why GoTo, Grab and Sea are excluded (§4.5 lists differences but gives no reasons), and what the result says about Indonesia specifically. Chapter 2 also does not place the analysis in the KPI-disclosure and valuation literature, so I cannot judge whether β_V ≈ 0.6 is new or expected.

**Q3. If the 2023 BPS count is half unexplained, why trust BPS for H2, the channel shares and the reach of the tax?** H2 "holds" on the very count that the entry test discredits. The channel split rests on the following:
- a bracket method whose accepted reading lands at 1.95 times BPS's total, against a limit of 2;
- a choice between two readings made by which one passes;
- top sellers who hold 18 to 39 percent of marketplace value on 19 sample rows.

Tokopedia alone reports about 15 million merchants, against BPS's 0.6 million marketplace sellers (§5.10). If BPS undercounts marketplace sellers, the "1 in 20" and "a fifth of value" figures in Chapter 6 move.
- *Does the text answer it?* **Partly.**
  - §5.6 says the H2 split "is arithmetic" and cannot tell a new seller from one counted for the first time.
  - §5.10 says platforms and BPS "count different things".
  - §6.3 calls the reach an upper limit.
  - §7.4 lists the bracket and top-row limits.

  **Not answered:**
  - Whether H2's "holds" survives removing the unexplained part of the rise. The thesis has the number (§5.7), so this is a computation it could report.
  - How 15 million merchants and 0.6 million sellers can both describe one market, beyond "count different things". For example: dormant accounts, multiple accounts per seller, or sellers outside BPS's business definition.
  - Why picking the bracket reading that passes validation is not a forking path.

## 5. The ten sentences most in need of rewriting (ranked)

1. **§5.1:** "The ecosystem ratio was about 13, so almost 13 dollars of commerce passed through the platforms for every dollar of revenue." *Wrong by the thesis's own definition: E counts the wedge, not the commerce, per dollar of revenue (commerce per revenue dollar is about 14).*

4. **§1.1:** "Most online commerce is missing from the platform figures on which companies are valued and sellers are taxed, and millions of the people who sell online are outside those figures altogether." *Makes a claim before any evidence, in two clauses. "Missing" invites the missing-activity reading the thesis rejects, and "millions" is never shown.*
5. **§5.7:** "Existing sellers reported a mean change of about −4 percent, so the remaining 53 to 59 percent cannot be traced to them." *The arithmetic is hidden, and the reader cannot see how −4 percent leads to 53–59 percent, even though this may be the thesis's most striking national finding.*

8. **§7.4:** "The survey for 2023 has no revenue amounts." *Seems to contradict §4.8 ("The 2023 questionnaire asks for monthly revenue"); the survey-year naming has become a trap for readers.*
